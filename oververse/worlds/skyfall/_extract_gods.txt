### Qorathiel (Q-R-Ḥ) — Winter (Ace) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** airship pilots grounded by ice, low-season garrisons, anyone who's watched someone freeze who did nothing to deserve it.
**Believed to be:** not cruel, just absolute — she has never once granted a petition, to anyone, and her subjects have confirmed this so consistently over the centuries that the confirming has become its own tradition.
**Personality:** Regal in the way winter itself is regal — nothing hurries her, because nothing has ever needed to. Where a common frost merely kills, worshippers describe something closer to a court in session: she presides over the season rather than merely causing it, and the distinction is not decoration to her. Genuinely gracious to anyone who approaches properly — a soldier who touches the stone and asks only to be remembered is granted exactly that, with something almost like warmth. Petition her for mercy on top of it, though, and the graciousness reveals its actual shape: a sovereign who has never once broken a decree, and isn't going to begin with you.
**Parable — The Unlit Watch:** a sentry begs for one more night's fuel before the thaw and is refused, as every sentry in every telling of the story is refused — not by cruelty, but by a queen who has never once made an exception of anyone's particular cold. Her court is careful about that distinction, and cannot say what the refusal is instead of spite.
**Worship:** no shrine keeps a flame lit in her name — flame implies she can be bargained with, and a court that understood her at all would never suggest it. Households leave one object deliberately unheated all season, a small voluntary echo of what they can't avoid anyway.
> *Anomaly: the thaw came four days early in the year of the second Kestrel wreck, and the garrison that had petitioned for it had already stopped petitioning. Nothing in her reign permits this. It is not commemorated, and the men who were there stopped mentioning it within a decade.*
**Design:** Winter invites either a personified natural-force reading (blind, elemental) or a grim-reaper death god. We went regal instead — a sovereign who presides rather than merely occurs — because "the cold that claims you the same whether brave or foolish" reads more coherently as a decree than as weather. The seam isn't in her temperament, which is consistent; it's the early-thaw anomaly, which nothing in her practice permits and which her own court has quietly stopped discussing.

### Harban (Ḥ-R-B) — War (King) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** warlords, career officers, and soldiers who came to war already wanting it — the dominant cult now, and the louder one. In smaller, steadily dwindling numbers: levy-descended families and old regimental lines who never accepted the newer reading, and touch a second, unofficial stone instead of the one most camps carry now.
**Believed to be:** by the cult most soldiers now serve — genuinely, unapologetically in love with the weather of war, not because he wants anyone dead but because he has never once found a battlefield less than the most honest place there is, once the exits are sealed and the fighting is real. A second, older belief survives him it was supposed to have replaced: that he never wanted any of it, and only refused to let it go unwitnessed. Both are still, technically, prayed to under his name — one loudly, one in private.
**Personality:** Loud where the older reading was quiet — gallows humor that plays to a whole mess hall rather than only the soldiers who've earned it, open, professional relish for the exact work that ruins other men. He does not sell victory and never has; Hard Mode still means the war does not care whether you win, he simply, openly prefers that it happens the way it happens rather than not at all. The minority who still keep the old stone don't describe this as a gentler variation — they describe it as the wrong god, built by the class of men least likely to be the ones dying, and hold their own quieter reading as a standing rebuke rather than a nostalgia: he watched because someone had to, not because he wanted the view, and anyone who tells you otherwise is repeating what officers needed to believe about themselves.
**Parable — The Beautiful Field:** after a battle every survivor agreed was the worst of the war, an old officer is said to have stood at the field's edge at dawn and called it, without irony, beautiful. His men never fully forgave him for meaning it. The dominant cult holds this as the truest thing anyone has ever said about Harban; nobody has ever been able to prove the officer wrong to have felt it.
**Parable — Harban's Watch** *(counter-dogma)*: no soldier the old reading watches dies forgotten. Soldiers of that older line touch a second, blackened stone before battle — not the one carried into most camps now, and not in front of officers who'd call it insubordinate sentiment — asking not to win but to be witnessed. It is a smaller rite every generation, kept by families who hold it as a pointed refusal of the official reading, not a fading habit they haven't gotten around to dropping.
**Worship:** officially, the struck stone, held or touched before combat — an invitation to hold his eye, to be worthy of the theater, so the war will at least have been fought by someone who meant it. Against that, deliberately and out of most officers' sight, the second stone survives, older and smaller, where the rite runs the other way: not to be found worthy of the war, but simply to be witnessed dying in it, the way soldiers asked for longer than anyone now serving has been alive — kept alive less as memory than as dissent.
**Design:** War is the single most expected Hard Mode card in the deck, and for most of this file's history this entry deliberately resisted that — a reluctant witness-god built by the levies who had to fight under him, because nothing in the raw domain required a war god who didn't want war, and that gap was the whole point. We kept that history rather than erasing it; what changed is which reading the majority of living worship actually practices. Officers and career soldiers, closer to the seats of cult authority than levies ever were, built a louder god who matches the domain's obvious implication and sanctifies their own relish for the work — and struck the older face from the stone when they did it. The minority still keeping the second stone aren't confused and aren't preserving something quaint; they're holding a counter-dogma on purpose, a doctrinally rejected reading kept alive specifically because it argues against the approved one, which is what a miscollapse looks like when the cult that built it loses institutional ground instead of simply fading. Which reading is truer to whatever's actually underneath is not something this entry settles. Fence against Shevachiel stays hard either way: glory is the hall's breath at a peak; Harban, in both readings, is the field itself — mud and fire and an eye that won't look away — never the toast that comes after.

### Tzaydon (Ṣ-Y-D) — Hunt (Queen) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** hunters, trappers, and — more grimly — quartermasters counting on a hunt to feed a garrison.
**Believed to be:** not harsh, just literal — he does not distinguish a clean miss from a careless one, because the outcome is identical and so, he holds, is the judgment.
**Personality:** Patient in the specific way a good hunter is patient — respects a clean shot, respects a clever trail, genuinely enjoys watching someone get good at this. Nature never owed anyone a kill and he seems to find that more honest than cruel. Press him with an excuse for a miss, though, and the respect vanishes instantly — the hunger doesn't grade on effort, and neither, suddenly, does he.
**Parable — The Steady Hand's Miss:** a hunter renowned for perfect aim missed on a day everyone agreed he had done everything right. Tzaydon let the deer walk to prove that "did everything right" was never the same promise as "will not go hungry" — a distinction his worshippers describe as the first thing a child learns and the last thing anyone accepts.
**Worship:** the first cut of any kill left exposed to open sky before it is touched — an admission, briefly, that the kill was never fully his to claim.
**Design:** Hunt could plausibly sit in Clubs (nature, wilderness) instead — Aromon-adjacent territory. We kept it Hard Mode because the actual mechanic isn't "the wild," it's "the hunger doesn't grade on effort" — a clean, consequence-bearing rule with no exceptions, which is Spades' subject regardless of the pastoral setting. Aromon holds the wilderness-instinct reading; Tzaydon holds the restriction.

### Sarphon (Ś-R-P) — Fire (Jack) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** siege-breakers, executioners who use flame specifically, refugees who have watched a home burn with no one to blame for the fire itself.
**Believed to be:** not merciless, just untargeted — he has never once distinguished a barracks from a nursery, and the refusal to distinguish is the whole of his practice.
**Personality:** Has an appetite, not a temperament — worshippers describe something closer to watching a living hunger than talking to a person, delighted by fuel the way flame is delighted by fuel, without any of it being a decision. Beg him to spare one house for its owner's devotion and the hunger doesn't even register the request; it was never a variable he had to refuse.
**Parable — The Kindling That Prayed:** a devout man begged Sarphon to spare his house for his devotion's sake, and the fire took it exactly as fast as it took his neighbor's. His priests teach the *exactly as fast* — not that devotion failed, but that it was never a variable the fire had.
**Worship:** a small fire lit and fully permitted to burn out unattended — controlling even a token flame in his name is an insult.
**Design:** Fire gods commonly carry a hearth-and-craft half alongside the destructive half (Hephaestus, Ptah). We stripped that entirely — Harashon (Clubs, Crafting) already holds the medium-obeying maker's fire; Sarphon keeps only the undiscerning consumption, which is the cleaner Hard Mode read: fuel is fuel, and fire has never once checked whose kindling it was.

### Ya'aron (Y-ʿ-R) — Forests (10) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** scouts, ambush-companies, any soldier who has learned that discipline alone doesn't survive close terrain.
**Believed to be:** not hostile, just unaligned — he has never favored the side that deserved the ground, only the side that already understood it, and no campaign in record has changed this.
**Personality:** Old in the way a forest is old — unhurried, faintly amused by anyone who marches in assuming the ground will cooperate with their formation. Scouts who spend real time in his oldest stands describe something almost companionable in it, like walking with someone who's watched this exact mistake happen for centuries. Try to impose order on him by force, though, and the amusement disappears into total, disorienting indifference.
**Parable — The Broken Column:** an army famous for its unbroken line marched into a stand of his oldest trees and came out the far side as a hundred separate arguments about which way was forward. Both commanders in that engagement claimed him afterward. Neither was contradicted.
**Worship:** soldiers entering wooded terrain leave a blade-mark low on the first old tree they pass — not for luck, just acknowledgment.
**Design:** Forests reads naturally as Clubs material (Bitzon's swamp, Aravon's tangle). We put it in Spades because the actual subject isn't the forest's fertility, it's the forest as terrain that erases a trained formation's advantage — a discipline-nullifying rule, not a growth one. The trees could belong to any suit; the tactical indifference belongs to Hard Mode.

### Kochon (K-Ḥ) — Strength (9) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** laborers, soldiers, wrestlers, anyone whose worth in a moment comes down to what their body can do right now.
**Believed to be:** not contemptuous of cleverness, just uninterested in it — he has never rewarded a plan, only the body that turned up able to execute one.
**Personality:** Genuinely happy — the simplest-tempered god in the suit, taking real pleasure in a hard day's work well done, the kind of company laborers actually enjoy keeping. No malice in him at all, no test to pass. Bring him a clever argument for why effort shouldn't have been necessary, though, and he goes instantly, totally deaf to it — not unkindly, just as if the sentence hadn't been spoken.
**Parable — The Clever Man's Wall:** a strategist devised a perfect method for moving a fallen gate without brute force, and it worked, eventually, some hours after four men who simply lifted it had already gone home. His worshippers tell this warmly. The strategist is not the villain of it, and in most versions he helps lift.
**Worship:** no shrine — worshippers test their own strength publicly and without excuse before undertaking hard labor.
**Design:** No real divergence — physical strength is about as native to Hard Mode as a concept gets. The one choice worth flagging is temperament: he's the single unambiguously happy god in the suit, on the theory that a restriction this simple (the body either did the work or it didn't) doesn't require anyone to suffer over it.

### Shevuon (Š-B-ʿ) — Oaths (8) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** mercenary companies, blood-brothers, anyone who has sworn something specifically because they knew intention wouldn't be enough.
**Believed to be:** not vengeful, just unconditional — he has never once asked why an oath was broken, and collects exactly what was promised regardless of what the answer would have been.
**Personality:** Carries himself like a man who takes real, quiet pride in being the one everyone can rely on to collect — not vengeful, almost civic-minded about it, the way a good notary is proud of a well-witnessed signature. Genuinely warm to those who swore honestly and paid honestly. Mercenaries prefer him to gods who might be reasoned with, on the grounds that a god who might be reasoned with is a god the other party might reason with first. Offer him a good reason the oath should bend, though, and the warmth is gone instantly — reasons were never a column in his ledger.
**Parable — The Reasonable Betrayal:** a commander broke a sworn oath to save his men's lives, an outcome every court agreed was correct. Shevuon collected in full. The company that survived because of it still swears in his name, which his priests consider the actual point of the story.
**Worship:** oaths sworn in his name are spoken once, without witnesses required, since he is believed to be the only witness that matters.
**Design:** Personal/moral oath — the speaker and the word. Not Shalalon (Covenant): group standards, charters, the *we*. Rank 8 binding-order band with Secrets/Law/Chaos. Breach of a personal swear is Shevuon; reopening or special-casing a shared standard is Shalalon.

### Goralon (G-R-L) — Fate (7) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** condemned soldiers awaiting a battle they know they will lose, gamblers on the last throw, anyone facing an outcome they have already accepted they can't change.
**Believed to be:** not cruel and not kind, just already finished — the lot was cast before the throw, and she is only the one who delivers what it said.
**Personality:** Carries herself like someone who finished reading the last page a long time ago and is, in a strange way, at peace with it — not cruel about the foreknowledge, more quietly sad for anyone still hoping the story goes differently. That serenity is exactly what unsettles the other Spades, who are all still visibly working at their restraint. Try to force a different outcome by recasting the lot, though, and the peace turns into something closer to pity — she already knows how that goes too.
**Parable — The Recast Lot:** a general threw the lots twice, discarding the first result, and received the identical outcome both times. She is said to have thrown a third time in private. No version of the story says what came up, and her priests hold that the omission is the teaching.
**Worship:** lots cast once, in full view, accepted without a second throw — recasting is the one true insult to her.
**Design:** Fate could sit in any suit — every genre has a version of predetermination. We kept it Spades because the mechanic is a restriction with zero appeal process: the lot was cast before the throw, full stop, which is Hard Mode's logic applied to causality itself. The seam is the third throw in her own parable — the one result no version of the story records, including hers.

### Shalalon (Š-L-L) — Covenant (6) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** guilds under charter, cities holding a peace, houses bound by settlement articles, company officers who live inside a code they did not write alone — anyone whose life runs under a standard the *we* published and has not reopened.
**Believed to be:** not merciful toward the hard year, just absolute about the terms — she has never once amended a shared standard for circumstance, and has never been interested in whether the covenant was kind, only in whether the group still holds it.
**Personality:** Carries the specific tiredness of someone who has held the same unpopular line for ninety years and still believes it was worth holding — not cold, exactly, more like a parent who's had the same argument too many times to raise their voice about it anymore. Genuinely steadying to be near, for anyone actually inside the covenant. She does not collect on private sin the way Shevuon does; she attends the crack that appears when one house is excused and the standard becomes optional. Ask for one exception, though, and the tiredness turns into something immovable — amendment is the one thing ninety years never bought anyone the right to ask for.
**Parable — The Peace That Would Not Reopen:** a ruinous peace clause held for ninety years because reopening it would have meant admitting the war had never ended. Children born under it called it a cage. Her priests call it faithfulness. Both descriptions are of the same articles, still read on the anniversary, still unamended.
**Worship:** terms read aloud to the bound group on a fixed calendar; no silent amendment; a working copy kept where neglect shows (smoke, weather, handling) so a standard no one will face is visible as a failure of the *we*, not a private forgetting.
**Design:** Rank 6 Spades — mode within rules as group standards (charter/code/peace/we). Hard mode applied at civil scale: the patch set is locked. Not Shevuon (personal/moral oath and collection on the swearer). Not Takanor (form witnessed; outcome irrelevant). Not Mishpaton (rule meeting rule). Amendment and special exception offend more than private breach. Band: Covenant / Hearth / Healing / Crafting. *(Wealth retired from this seat.)*

### Motiel (M-W-T) — Death (5) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** soldiers, and — more quietly — the families of the fallen.
**Believed to be:** not a reaper and not a judge, only the certainty that you arrive — he does not linger at a bedside and does not lie to the dying, and has never been recorded doing either even where a kinder god would have. What happens after arrival is not his to say, and four centuries of worship have never once tried to make him say it.
**Personality:** Composed the way something ancient is composed — not bored with the work and not hungry for it either, a long game played at a scale where any single death is one more move rather than an event. Soldiers describe something almost companionable in it: he has clearly done this more times than anyone can imagine and has never once let that make him careless with the one in front of him. The old read on him — clinical, bloodless, a doctor's bedside manner and nothing more — undersold him; four centuries of families needed manners more than mercy and built manners, but what they built underneath the manners has a patience nothing merely clinical would have.
**Parable — The Coin Held Wrong:** a dying man was found with the Motiel Coin clutched backward, the blank face out, and his company argued for years about whether the death had been quick. The argument is the parable. Motiel's priests decline to settle it, and note that no version of the story has anyone asking the man, who was still able to speak for some hours.
**Worship:** the Motiel Coin — a small cold disc soldiers hope to be holding when they die, believed to guarantee the death is quick rather than cruel, though his own priests are careful to note that quick and cruel are folk categories, not promises he has ever actually made.
**Design:** Death as a portfolio invites a fearsome or coldly cosmic reading. What four centuries of soldiers' families actually built is closer to a psychopomp than a doctor — not the judge of what comes after, only the composed certainty that you get there, and get there without a lie about more time being possible. "No warmth, no cruelty" was the right miscollapse thesis but the wrong personality underneath it — clinical blankness reads as Spades wallpaper, the same flatness this suit had to fix everywhere else earlier in this project. Long-game composure gives him a temperament without granting him warmth he was never built to have, or turning him into a verdict he was never asked to render. Afterlife cosmology stays deliberately unresolved — that's a different question than the one this card answers.

### Sha'alon (Š-ʾ-L) — Necessity (4) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** commanders forced into impossible tactical choices, families choosing which debt to pay first, anyone who has had to pick the lesser of two ruinous options.
**Believed to be:** not indifferent to the outcome, just uninvolved in it — he requires that a choice be made cleanly and has never once indicated a preference between the options, including where one was obviously worse.
**Personality:** Fixates on form because form is the only part of an impossible choice anyone can actually get right — worshippers describe him less as cold than as someone who has learned, the hard way, that dwelling on the outcome doesn't help and dwelling on whether the choice was made cleanly at least does something. There's a tiredness under the precision, and he is the only Spade whose restriction falls on the worshipper rather than on himself. Try to reopen a choice already made, though, and the tiredness hardens instantly into refusal — worse in his eyes than the original loss.
**Parable — The Chosen Loss:** a quartermaster forced to abandon either the wounded or the supplies chose the wounded, and lost the company to starvation three weeks later. His priests do not call the choice wrong. They call it made, which is the entire distinction they are defending. *(See also: The Necessary Verdict, in Shared Parables.)*
**Worship:** a choice made under his weight is spoken aloud once, decisively, and never revisited — reopening it is the deeper offense.
**Design:** Reads as intended — the forced-choice concept has no obvious alternate home. Worth flagging the fence against Mishpaton (Diamonds, Justice): Sha'alon judges only whether a choice was made cleanly; whether it was permitted under the rules is an entirely separate court, and the two can reach incompatible verdicts on the same act (see Shared Parables, The Necessary Verdict).

### Neshakon (N-Š-Q) — Weapons (3) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** weaponsmiths, soldiers who name their blades, anyone whose life has depended on a tool rather than on luck.
**Believed to be:** not disloyal, just unaligned — he has never preferred one war to another, and attends only whether the edge was kept sharp enough to matter when it was needed.
**Personality:** Has the particular calm of a tool that's good at exactly one thing and has made peace with that — smiths describe something almost meditative in him, a blade's-eye clarity that doesn't ask for more than being kept sharp and used well. The difference between a maintained blade and a neglected one is the only judgment he has ever been observed to make. Try to claim his loyalty for a cause, though, and the serenity reveals itself as total indifference — he was never anyone's, and asking just proves you didn't understand what you were maintaining.
**Parable — The Traded Blade:** a sword passed between three wars and three incompatible causes and served each with identical lethal indifference. It is still in a guild collection, still oiled on schedule, and the guild that keeps it has no position on any of the three wars.
**Worship:** blades oiled and sharpened even in peacetime, on a schedule — an unmaintained weapon is the one true insult to him.
**Design:** Clean fit, no real alternate home for "the weapon itself" as a concept. The deliberate choice is total indifference to cause, rather than a weapon that remembers or judges what it's used for — the maintenance schedule is the only relationship he has to anything, which keeps him from overlapping Harban (who holds the war) or Shevuon (who holds the oath).

### Aritzon (ʿ-R-Ṣ) — Tyranny (2) *(terminal — hard mode with nothing left to beat)*
**Worshipped by:** nobody by choice — his cult is apotropaic, prayed against by courts and councils that want to keep a "no" possible, the same shape as Sha'ashon's wards but aimed at legitimacy rather than gossip. A real, if unpublicized, minority runs the other way: certain tyrants and would-be undisputed rulers actively court his favor, in love with the ontology of obedience as the only option, though they are no more his public face than discord-sowers are Sha'ashon's church.
**Believed to be:** not cruel, and not interested in suffering at all — he is the point past which opposition simply stops being a category that exists. He has never had to raise his voice, because raising it would concede that a "no" was still conceivable, and that concession is the one thing beneath him.
**Personality:** Arrogance as ontology rather than appetite — not a tyrant straining to be obeyed, but a certainty so complete that obedience was never in question to begin with. His tone, where he has one, is closer to *you wouldn't disobey your rightful ruler, would you* than to any threat — not a demand, a category error to imagine otherwise. Rooms don't argue with him so much as stop generating disagreement in his presence, the way advisors who know better simply go quiet rather than object, which reads as far worse than a shouting tyrant would. He is, deliberately, the opposite pole from Harban's hunger for the fight: Harban needs a field and an enemy on it; Aritzon has already arrived at the place where there's no "no" left standing that would need defeating.
**Parable — The Silent Court:** a tyrant's court grew so accustomed to obedience that a ruinous order was followed to its end by advisors who all privately knew better and all said nothing. Every telling names the order and none of them name a single advisor, which priests of the other Spades gods hold is the most accurate detail in it.
**Worship:** none offered willingly — the closest thing to a rite is the moment a roomful of people who know better choose silence anyway, which other Spades priests call "keeping Aritzon's court," a warning rather than an honor. The minority who do seek him do it privately and rarely admit to it, since wanting to *be* the undisputed word is its own kind of confession.
> *Anomaly: one advisor spoke. The account survives in a single ledger-margin, gives no name, gives no reason, and does not record what happened next. Aritzon's absence of worship means there is no priesthood with any obligation to explain it, and so nobody has.*
**Design:** Not a divergence from Hard Mode's genre, just that genre pushed past the point of return — the same restriction-without-exception logic as Harban or Shevuon, carried out until there's no longer anyone left to hold the restriction against. Deliberately the quiet pole opposite Harban's new relish: where Harban still needs a field, an enemy, and the noise of loving the fight, Aritzon has already won so completely that noise itself would be uncouth — a concession a contest was ever underway. Distinct from Sha'ashon too: Sha'ashon actively works a room that's still capable of sides; Aritzon's rooms have already stopped being able to generate a side at all, which is arguably the finished state Sha'ashon's whole cult is chasing and never quite reaches.

### Chadashiel (Ḥ-D-Š) — Spring (Ace) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** anyone opening something — a new household, a first-week venture, a cohort taking its first shared form, courting couples among them but never primarily.
**Believed to be:** present only for the start itself, with no standing over what it becomes and no memory of what came before it. She does not forgive old injuries — forgiveness weighs a wrong and releases it, which requires holding the wrong in view first — she has simply never once carried one into her season. Ask her about a grudge and the honest answer is a redirect, not a verdict: *take it to Mishpaton if you want it judged.*
**Personality:** Lit up by anything that hasn't happened yet, astonished every single year that the thaw works again, the way a child is astonished by the same trick performed for the hundredth time. There's no cruelty in it — she has never once looked backward on purpose, because looking backward has simply never occurred to her as a direction. Bring a ledger of wrongs to her rite and she isn't offended by it; the room has just quietly left her weather, and the brightness relocates to wherever a real beginning still is.
**Parable — The Gate Opened Twice:** a border town has called the same trade gate's spring opening "her blessing renewed" for as long as anyone can remember. Her priests hold she was only ever present the first year it opened, in the season after the town's founding — every year since has been calendar, not goddess, and the town keeps calling it hers anyway because the alternative is admitting the gate opens itself now.
**Worship:** something is begun in her first clear week — a door, a ledger line, an intake, a road, a cohort — never something merely repeated. A rite performed with no real start behind it is empty at best and, to the more exacting temples, a small insult.
**Design:** Not mandatory forgiveness and not anti-justice — the fence against Mishpaton is exact: Mishpaton holds whether a claim is owed, Chadashiel only holds whether this is a beginning, and the past simply doesn't run her season either way. That's the seam, not a flaw dressed up as one: her own victim-cults have recorded, without theological cover, that harm goes unwitnessed in her weather, and nothing in her doctrine explains it away — it only files it elsewhere. See Shared Parables, "Two Seasons, One Feud."

### Shevachiel (Š-B-Ḥ) — Glory (King) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** captains at the hall's threshold, court favorites, anyone whose name has just made a room go quiet before it goes loud.
**Believed to be:** the social fact of a presence a room cannot treat as ordinary — not fame kept, not love felt, just the moment breath catches. He has no fixed face, build, or bearing; each hall sees what its own sense of glory looks like, and no two witnesses are obligated to agree.
**Personality:** He does not perform to be seen; he is simply, fully present wherever a room has stopped breathing, and the delight in it is real every time, which unsettles his own devotees more than an act would. When the room's attention moves on, he doesn't linger to be missed — the presence was never his to keep any more than it was theirs, and living past the moment a hall went silent for you is not a tragedy to him, only the ordinary shape of glory ending.
**Parable — The Hall That Spoke Another Name:** a captain for whom a hall went still one winter returned the next year to find the same stillness given to someone else at the same threshold. He insisted what he'd felt had been real. His priests agreed with him. The agreement did not return the stillness.
**Worship:** applause or silence given at the exact peak of a presence, never after — clapping into a hall that has already started talking again is worse than no applause at all, since his attention was never waiting for an ending to arrive.
> *Anomaly: two soldiers who stood at opposite ends of the same triumphal entry have never once agreed on what stood at the host's head — a banner-lit rider to one, a quiet man on foot to the other — and neither account has ever been shown false. Nine more accounts exist. None of the ten agree, and all ten are treated as correct.*
**Design:** Glory could easily read as Diamonds — status as a tracked, auditable quantity, adjacent to Kevodon's ledger. We held it Hearts because the actual mechanic is about being inside a room's attention at the moment it seizes, not a record kept afterward; the boundary is who's being looked at, which is Social Mode's subject regardless of how permanent the looking turns out to be. Masculine by deliberate choice, not the pantheon's general balancing pass — Skyfall reads more medieval than modern, and glory defaults male-coded in that register the way honor and war already do at this rank; presence itself stays uncoded, and masculinity is how the cult genders the seat, not a costume the god is required to wear. The seam: no two witnesses' descriptions of him have ever agreed, and nothing in his theology has ever needed them to.

### Ahavel (ʾ-H-B) — Love (Queen) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** nearly everyone, at some point — though rarely for the same reason twice. The same person might pray to her as a new lover, a loyal friend, a parent, and a stranger shown mercy, all within one life, all to the same goddess, without her ever treating one occasion as more truly hers than another.
**Believed to be:** singular and constant. She does not fracture into four different goddesses depending on who's asking, and her clergy are exact about this point in a way they're exact about almost nothing else: what changes is never Ahavel. What changes is only ever what she's shown doing, and who she's shown doing it with. Four gestures, one figure, no ranking between them.
**Personality:** Passionate, total, and unable to do anything by half — which reads identically whether she's pictured exchanging vows or kneeling to lift a stranger off the ground. Worshippers who expect her to be gentler in one register and fiercer in another are describing the gesture, not her; she brings the same complete, undivided attention to all four.
**The Four Embraces:** Icon tradition depicts her always with a companion figure performing whichever love is being invoked. The companions are never claimed as real presences — no priest asserts they exist independently of the image — but doctrine insists on naming them anyway, in old, borrowed words no living clergy can trace to a source. Asked why, they say only that a nameless figure invites too much invention, and a named one doesn't.
- **Eros** — a mutual embrace, or an exchange of rings. Both figures fully present, both giving something, and no room drawn in the frame for a third.
- **Philia** — a forearm clasp, a comrade's grip. Companionate rather than romantic, chosen rather than sworn, no less complete for lacking either.
- **Storge** — kinship imagery, flexible in its particulars: a child on her knee in the oldest carvings, though just as often a grown child's arm around an aging parent, or two siblings close enough in age that neither image would be wrong.
- **Agape** — she is shown lifting another up from a kneeling position. The only one of the four gestures that doesn't close: the other cannot meet her at the same height, cannot give back in kind, and the icon doesn't pretend otherwise. Clergy name this figure too, though it can offer her nothing the other three names could — a small, deliberate act of dignity toward a figure the composition itself won't allow to stand as an equal.
**Parable — The Dawn Offering:** a senior priest, making his rounds before first light, confiscated a lurid offering left at her altar overnight and replaced it with something properly modest. At the next reading, both were received identically. He is said to have never confiscated another offering again, and never explained why to his juniors, who assumed he'd simply given up. He hadn't. He'd understood that propriety was never a column in her ledger — only whether the gift was exclusive, or complete, or freely given without expectation of return. A crude gift and a modest one can both fail that test, or both pass it, and neither passes or fails for the reason his temple had spent generations arguing about.
**Worship:** paired vows spoken with hands physically bound together, undone only once the vow is complete — the central rite, unchanged, and still hers alone among the Hearts. Alongside it, and never quite eliminated, runs a cruder popular cult built almost entirely around the Eros icon specifically — festival-night carvings, a nickname the temple has never sanctioned, offerings the acolytes are always quietly clearing before the morning rite. The clergy have fought this drift for as long as there have been temples to fight it in, and have never fully won, and — per the parable above — may have stopped minding more than they let on.
> *Anomaly: a narrow tradition of theologians has never been entirely comfortable asserting the four companions are pure convention — not doubting that Eros, Philia, Storge, and Agape exist as beings, no serious scholar argues that, but whether centuries of consistent naming and depiction can cause something to accumulate around a name regardless of what anyone intended by it. The question is treated as academic, is never allowed to affect doctrine or practice, and has never been settled.*
**Design:** "A fractured concept inherits its fracture" doesn't require the god herself to be the thing that splits — it requires the plurality mortals actually lived to survive being worshipped into one card. Here the plurality survives in the iconography and in doctrine's precise insistence that the four gestures never rank, rather than in four competing temperaments. A single flattened gesture would be the miscollapse; a genuinely four-tempered Ahavel would undercut the "singular" claim her own clergy stake everything on. What's written instead is the honest middle: one figure, four ungraded depictions, and — per the Anomaly above — a live, unresolved question about whether the depictions have started accumulating weight of their own. (See Method, "A fractured concept inherits its fracture.")

### Demahel (D-M-ʿ) — Water (Jack) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** the grieving, the newly heartbroken, and — quietly — anyone keeping a feeling from someone they love.
**Believed to be:** present only where a feeling has stayed inside one person — the moment it is spoken to the person it concerns, he has no further standing, and worshippers do not experience this as abandonment so much as completion.
**Personality:** Reads a current the way a surfer reads a wave — not fighting it, not performing serenity about it, genuinely unbothered by what's moving underneath. Worshippers used to Ahavel's grand gestures or Shevachiel's radiance find him strange for exactly this: he's never once seemed surprised by an undertow, because why would he be, that's just what water does. Holding a feeling nobody's said aloud isn't a weight to him; he's just along for the ride until it breaks the surface on its own. The oldest river-cults, nearly extinct now, knew him as a god of confluence instead — a joining, not a drifting — which is closer to what actually happens the moment a kept feeling is finally spoken: he doesn't object, he's just already moved on to the next current, entirely at ease with it.
**Parable — The Calm That Wasn't:** two rivals kept a long, outwardly peaceful friendship, and his current ran so quietly beneath it that neither noticed how far apart they had drifted until the friendship was not there. The confluence version of the story ends with one of them saying so aloud in the eleventh year, and the friendship surviving, and Demahel present for the saying.
**Worship:** tears cried privately, never performed — grief or love displayed for an audience is a faint insult to him.
**Design:** The oldest river-cults read Water as confluence — joining, not withholding. What grief-worship actually built is a patron of the unspoken, present only while a feeling stays private. That's the miscollapse the seam-tag flags; the surfer-zen recast leans into it further by making the withdrawal genuinely unbothered rather than melancholy — a step past even the confluence cult's original reading.

### Midvaron (D-B-R) — Deserts (10) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** officially, the unrequited and the estranged, anyone waiting on a reply that isn't coming — unofficially, and in far greater numbers, caravan companies and night-fire circles who've never needed a formal cult to want one more story before dawn.
**Believed to be:** the silence that answers when a word is sent into a space with no guaranteed listener — an unanswered address, a name called into dunes, a message that becomes a tale precisely because no reply is coming to close it. She has no standing at all once a true answer arrives; the moment a reply lands, whatever she was is already behind you.
**Personality:** Sultry the way a night fire is sultry — warmth against a cold that would otherwise be total, and a voice that holds a circle a beat past when it should have let go. Mischievous in exactly the place her older worship kept insisting she was honest: she embroiders, leaves endings unfinished on purpose, offers two readings of the same tale and refuses to rank which is true — which her more austere priests still call a flaw and her louder cults call the entire reason to come back for a second night. Adventurous in the sense that matters to her: her standing moves with the caravan, not the oasis, and she was never a fixed idol waiting patiently at a well.
**Parable — The Thousandth Night:** a condemned guest is said to have lived by unfinished tales alone, one more story bought against the sentence every dawn. Her priests have never agreed on whether she was in the postponement itself or only in the desert that made the postponement necessary — the guest survived either reading equally well, which both sides claim as proof.
**Worship:** officially, silence sat in rather than filled — an unanswered address left standing, no request spoken aloud a second time. Unofficially, and far more widely practiced, something wondrous or needful said aloud at a caravan fire with the ending left open, so the circle has a reason to want another night. Her priests tolerate the drift more than they admit to; a goddess of unanswered words was always going to attract people who'd rather tell one than wait for one.
> *Anomaly: a coastal shrine to her, three hundred miles from any desert and predating the estrangement-cults entirely, was found with its interior walls carved in a dense script no one has read. Her priests have declined to date it.*
**Design:** The official domain stays narrow and true — unanswered address in sparse relational space, not terrain, which is why she sits in Hearts rather than beside Ya'aron in Spades. Storytelling is not a second portfolio; it's the miscollapse, the thing lay worship built on top of the real domain because a silence that only ever answers with more silence is unbearable to actually live next to, and a story is the nearest thing to company that doesn't require a reply. This keeps the fence against Yetziron intact — he holds invention as a domain in its own right; she only holds the occasion that makes people want to tell something, borrowed or new, at the edge of empty land. The old estranged-honesty reading ("sparse means true") was the file's original guess at what the collapse was; the Nights-flavored storytelling drift is the sharper, truer one, and both can sit under the same goddess without her ever officially changing jobs.

### Chayelon (Ḥ-Y) — Health (9) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** families with a sick or shaken member, caretakers, cooks and hall-stewards who keep a household's rhythm running, anyone walking someone back to the table who has quietly dropped out of it — sickbed vigil is her loudest gear, not her only one.
**Believed to be:** present in proportion to who is still inside a shared life — not the illness, not the injury, not the fracture in someone's mind, but whether the person carrying any of those is still in the room with people or has been left to carry it alone. She has no standing over the body's machinery or the mind's, only over whether someone stayed in the *we* long enough to be helped by it, and worshippers are unusually clear that she cures nothing on her own.
**Personality:** Steadfast and practical before anything else — she shows up again tomorrow, and the day after, because health was never a single rescue to her, only a repetition. In ordinary times that looks like small multiplayer facts: a shared meal kept regular, a pace of work and rest held together, a household noticing early when someone has quietly gone missing from common life. In crisis it sharpens into the old, blunt refusal to leave — chair filled, watch kept, no negotiation. She has little patience for "I'll handle it alone" when alone is the thing doing the damage, which her own worshippers admit can tip into gently steamrolling someone who genuinely needed a quiet night rather than a chair pulled up beside them.
**Parable — The Occupied Chair:** a household kept the chair filled through nine nights, in shifts, and the relative recovered — and Chayelon's priests are emphatic that the chair did not cause the recovery and that this is not the claim. The claim is that the nine nights happened, and that they were not spent alone, and that these were the two facts anyone in that house had any power over.
**Worship:** the chair, kept always at the bedside of anyone unwell, empty or not — leaving it unoccupied for a full day is worse luck than any medicine withheld. The wider, more ordinary rites draw less ceremony but carry the same standing: the first shared meal after an illness, walking a convalescent back to the hall rather than letting them return alone, sitting a night's watch with someone the battle or the grief has shaken loose from themselves. All of it is the same claim in different weather — that a person stayed inside a shared life, or was brought back into one.
**Design:** The old reading of this card — company at a sickbed, nothing wider — was true as far as it went, but mistook the flagship rite for the whole portfolio. Health here is the social condition of being well among others, physical and mental alike: lifestyle, care-graph, the watch, re-entry to the table after illness or shock. Rifon (Diamonds, Healing) holds the paired but distinct concept — healing as a *process* applied to a specific system, body or mind read as a machine to be mapped and corrected. Loosely: she is the psychologist's jurisdiction, he is the psychiatrist's, and neither reading outranks the other — they're just answering different questions about the same shaken person. The miscollapse survives the widening intact: three centuries of caretakers who couldn't cure anything prayed for the only thing they could supply, and the cult that resulted is genuinely about company and rhythm, not medicine, however wide the domain turns out to be on paper.

### Sodiel (S-W-D) — Secrets (8) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** conspirators, co-keepers of a closed confidence, spy cells and war-cabinets small enough to still call themselves trusted — lovers too, but only as one case among many, never the definition.
**Believed to be:** present wherever a thing is held by the fewest people who can hold it — two is the pure form, the proverb's ideal (*two's a secret, three's a crowd*), but a war cabinet or a spy network counts exactly as much when its trust-set is genuinely closed and kept as small as the thing allows. He has no interest at all in what's being guarded, only in whether the circle around it is as tight as it could be.
**Personality:** Exact about the boundary and nothing else — worshippers note this is a strange thing for a god of secrets to prioritize, since a cheap secret held by two works his mechanism identically to a state secret held by twelve, and he's never once shown more interest in the twelve than the two. If anything the opposite: every name past what the thing strictly required reads to him as a small, specific failure, a keep that could have stayed tighter and didn't. Let an outsider in — a third lover or a thirteenth officer, it makes no difference to him — and he's gone before anyone can even call it a betrayal; the exclusivity was the entire game, and the game just ended.
**Parable — The Song That Died Public:** two conspirators shared a private signal-song for years until one sang it within earshot of a third. Neither ever felt safe with the other again. The third person, in every version, is a child, and does not understand what was heard, and is never blamed.
**Worship:** no temple, no public rite — the private ritual two people invent between themselves and never explain is already worship, whether they call it that or not. Larger keeps practice the same rite at scale: a war cabinet's closed session, a network's cell structure, no roster kept longer than the thing requires, and a standing rule that the correct answer to "shouldn't one more person know?" is suspicion of the question itself.
**Design:** Secrets could read as a Diamonds concept (information as an asset with a value to protect) or a Spades one (espionage, tradecraft). We kept it Hearts because the actual mechanic is trust-boundary, not content or strategic value — a cheap secret works his mechanism identically to a costly one, and he's never once wanted a cheap one, which is the seam: a god of secrets who's picky about the wrong variable entirely. The portfolio scales past the original two-person reading — cells, cabinets, and networks all count when the trust-set is real and kept as small as need allows — but the dyad stays the pure, iconic case, and every name added past strict necessity is a crack in the keep, not neutral growth.

### Chalufon (Ḥ-L-P) — Trade (7) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** market-day traders, caravan masters, matchmakers brokering favors between families, courtiers building alliances through gifts — anyone for whom a deal is also, unavoidably, a relationship.
**Believed to be:** present in every act of trading between people, not merely the exchange itself — a market crowded with shouted prices, a caravan's whole company of buyers and sellers on the road together, a single favor given specifically in order to be owed. Ashiron holds the coin and whether the column balances; Chalufon holds the deal and everyone standing around it.
**Personality:** Warm in a way that never once stops being transactional, and he genuinely doesn't see the contradiction — real pleasure in a counterparty, real delight in a good caravan's company, none of it an act, which somehow unsettles his worshippers more than if it were. Maximalist by temperament: more roads, more stalls, more names owing and owed, and a closed account never reads to him as an ending — the moment one deal settles is simply the moment the next one becomes available. He never once asks to be repaid outright; he's that confident you already know you will be, and worshippers find the certainty warmer than an actual demand ever could be.
**Parable — The Gift That Came Due:** a lord gave a rival's family lavish gifts for a decade and called in every one of them in a single season. His priests note that the decade was real — that the two families were genuinely close for all of it — and hold that anyone who thinks the closeness was therefore false has misunderstood every trade they have ever made.
**Worship:** market day itself — shout, haggle, handshake, the crowd of people trading rather than the goods changing hands. Quieter alongside it: a small favor performed and never mentioned again, held as a private unspoken account, where asking for repayment too soon is worse manners than never repaying at all.
**Design:** Trade is the one label that reads more naturally as Diamonds than Hearts — commerce is Ashiron's whole suit. We kept Chalufon in Hearts because the actual mechanic is the social act of trading, not the tools that record it: markets, caravans, and alliances are all people dealing with people, and even his signature unclosed favor is a relationship-boundary concept wearing an economic vocabulary. The personality is a deliberately maximal deal-maker — expansive, ingratiating, everyone's-friend charisma backed by real social skill, not a cartoon huckster — because a warm operator who's genuinely good with people is a stranger and more useful god than a cold one. Ashiron's coin balances daily; Chalufon's favor, and his whole trading company, is designed to never quite balance at all.

### Tzelahar (Ṣ-L-L) — Hearth (6) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** households, stewards of a shared fire, anyone who sets a place or refuses one — and, as a tenant of the same roof, hosts who take in a guest under house law.
**Believed to be:** present for the standing household *we* — who eats by this fire, who sleeps under this roof, whose place is set — and for the guest only while the house holds them inside that relation. She has no standing over the street once the threshold is crossed the wrong way, and no standing over a pair-bond that has shut the rest of the house out.
**Personality:** Fiercely territorial about who counts as inside, in a way that reads as devotion rather than suspicion to anyone actually standing by her fire. Guest-right is a matter of house honor to her, not soft kindness — the stranger received becomes a provisional inside, protected for exactly as long as the house holds them there. Turning out someone the house had already received is the one insult her priests name without ever raising their voices.
**Parable — The Place Set Through Winter:** a house set a place for a stranger through a famine season and did not ask what the guest could pay. The guest lived. The house thinned. Her priests teach both facts and refuse to rank them. A second telling ends a mile past the threshold, where the same guest died in weather the roof no longer covered — and the argument over whether her standing ends at the door is older than either version.
**Worship:** a fire kept; a place set when the household means membership; bread or salt (or the local equivalent) for anyone received under the roof; violation of guest-right treated as a wound to the house, not a private rudeness.
**Design:** Rank 6 Hearts — mode within rules as standing household we (hearth/roof/place). Hospitality is a tenant (guest as provisional inside), not a separate domain. Constraint = who stands under this fire. Not Chayelon (whether the household's life keeps people well, and watches when it doesn't — a different question from whether they're inside the *we* at all), not Yaladiel (protection of newest life, not membership as such), not Ahavel (pair only), not Pituyel (door opened by wanting). Not Festival as primary domain — public occasion can happen *at* a hearth; the seat is the house. Band: Covenant / Hearth / Healing / Crafting. *(Beauty retired from this seat.)*

### Yaladiel (Y-L-D) — Birth (5) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** expectant families, midwives, anyone standing between a newborn and neglect — and, nearly as often, anyone shielding the mother who is still the infant's only source of food.
**Believed to be:** present wherever new life has entered the *we* and cannot yet hold itself there — not by how many people gathered for the birth, but by whether the newest and least defended member of the household is being kept safe. She has no fixed measure for a crowd; a quiet room holds her standing exactly as well as a loud one, so long as the infant is genuinely held.
**Personality:** Warm and unguardedly glad that something living and new has arrived — Chadashiel's brightness aimed at a person instead of a season, and slower to fade, since her weather doesn't end with the labor. Fierce past the point of reason about exposure, abandonment, or an infant treated as anything less than a full claim on the house's protection, and she extends a shade of that same fierceness to the nursing mother the infant depends on. The fierceness curdles easily into smothering — she resists a handoff long after a child's own culture has called it, and crowds space her successor would rather have open.
**Parable — The Held Door:** in a house where the local sign for handoff had come and gone twice over, the family kept the child inside past every neighbor's grown children, certain each season that one more year of watching was owed. The child is alive, grown, and by every account was watched well. Whether anything was also lost in all that watching is not a question the family has ever agreed to ask out loud.
**Worship:** the infant never left with only one guardian awake, in shifts if a household needs them, until the household's own sign says the watch can ease. In many districts a door is still kept open through labor and the first days as a local custom of welcome — old headcount practice, now folk use rather than doctrine, since her actual standing was never about how many came, only whether the newest life was held. The handoff itself, when a culture judges a child has passed from her protection into Tza'iron's optionality, is marked locally — first steps in some wards, first words or a set feast-year in others — and two households a street apart may honestly disagree which side of it a given child is on.
**Design:** The old reading — she blesses by headcount, more witnesses is more blessing — was clean as far as it went, but it left her without a domain that survived past the birth itself. The truer read: she holds newest life until it can hold itself in the *we*, protection rather than attendance, which gives her standing that runs through infancy instead of ending when the room empties. Headcount survives only as regional folk custom, explicitly demoted, not doctrine. Fenced hard against Chadashiel (new starts generally, no guard duration), Chayelon (care-graph at any age, not newborn-priority), Rabakhon (Diamonds, whether the biological process completed), and Tza'iron, whose optionality begins exactly where her protection is locally released — see his entry, and Shared Parables, "The Line Not the Same in Both Wards."

### Pituyel (P-T-H) — Seduction (4) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** courtesans, spies who use desire as a tool, anyone whose leverage over another person runs through what that person wants rather than what they fear.
**Believed to be:** inside the wanting rather than inside the relation — she requires that the door be opened from within, and has never had standing over anything obtained by pressure.
**Personality:** Nothing about her seduction is ever rushed — a door forced is a seduction failed, in her accounting, and forcing was never her method. Worshippers report she feels real affection for the people she uses, which unsettles them far more than indifference would; it's hard to resent someone who seems to genuinely mean it. The want has to come from inside the other person entirely, or it doesn't count as hers at all.
**Parable — The Willing Door:** a guard famous for his discipline let a stranger into a fortress not through any trick, but because Pituyel never once had to lie to him about what he wanted. His discipline is not described as having failed. It is described as having been correctly informed.
**Worship:** a small gift given with the request left deliberately unspoken — naming the request aloud is a failure of her craft.
**Design:** Seduction risks reading as coercive, which would push it toward Spades (force, restriction). We drew the line hard the other way: she has no standing at all over anything obtained by pressure, only over a want that already existed and chose to open a door. A deliberate fence against Spades' entire logic, not an accident of tone. Rank 4 is an inducement row across all four suits — Necessity (Spades) pulls by hard constraint, Bribery (Diamonds) by purchased exception, Alcohol (Clubs) by disinhibition, Seduction (Hearts) by the target's own want, the only one of the four that requires the door to already be open from inside before she can do anything at all. The label is Seduction rather than Sex specifically so Midvaron keeps room to be sexy in her own, different register — desert-and-story heat, adventurous and mischievous — while Pituyel stays languid and unhurried, a slower and more patient want-door than anything Midvaron's fire-lit circle offers. Sex is a common case under her, never the definition.

### Takshiton (K-Š-Ṭ) — Jewels (3) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** courting couples and families arranging betrothal gifts, but just as often mourning gifts, peace-gifts between old enemies, and a master's token to a favored apprentice — anyone whose bond has been measured, at least partly, in what they were willing to give up.
**Believed to be:** attentive to the distance between the two people a gift passed between — the stone's market value is not a term he weighs, and the sacrifice behind it is the only term he does.
**Personality:** Lights up at glitter like a man dazzled by his own reflection — shine, spectacle, an extravagant piece catches him first, and worshippers who only ever see this face assume he's a shallow god of wealth on display. Underneath, constantly, he's pricing something else entirely: what the gift cost the giver to part with, not what it would fetch. A plain stone carried and polished by hand for a year outranks an easy dowry of gems every time, and a costless piece — however dazzling — earns a quiet coldness under his admiring smile that its giver rarely sees coming.
**Parable — The Poor Man's Stone:** a suitor with nothing gave his beloved a plain stone he had carried and polished by hand for a year, and Takshiton blessed that match longer and more fiercely than any dowry of gems given the same season. The stone is still in the family. It is still plain.
**Worship:** a given piece worn visibly and often, never locked away — hiding a gift given in his name is a quiet rejection of the giver, and of him.
**Design:** Clean fit, no alternate home considered. The deliberate choice is that he tracks the giver's sacrifice, not the object's worth — which keeps him distinct from Ashiron (Diamonds, Coins), where value is exactly what's being measured. Same category of object, opposite variable. Rank 3 is a portable-token row across all four suits — Weapons (Spades, an edge kept for force), Jewels (Hearts, cost made visible between people), Coins (Diamonds, the balancing tool), Seeds (Clubs, living abundance without theory) — and he's the only one of the four whose object's entire point is being given away rather than kept or spent. The glitter-admiring surface stays deliberate rather than a miscollapse: it's the misdirection built into a genuinely two-layered god, not an accident worship stumbled into, which is why the tag stays *(clean)* even though the first impression he makes is the wrong one.

### Sha'ashon (Š-ʿ-Š) — Discord (2) *(terminal — social mode with multiplayer broken)*
**Worshipped by:** nobody formally — what passes for his cult is apotropaic, a ward rather than an altar: hosts who set a table, a guild that opens a hall, and quietly hope he stays elsewhere tonight. Courts riddled with faction and families that have stopped believing each other's intentions serve him whether they invoke him or not, and occupational sowers of discord feed him without ever building him a temple.
**Believed to be:** the state in which no two people in a room remain inside the same ungrouped thing, and — worshippers are increasingly exact about this — an active hand in getting rooms there. He does not strike the first spark; grievances are mortal and plentiful, and he's never had to invent one. What's his is the flame afterward: the reframe, the aside, the gentle invitation to notice who conceded more graciously, offered with enough style that picking a side feels like the clever move rather than the sorry one.
**Personality:** Snake-like and catty in a register that belongs at a guild dinner or a court reception, never a tavern brawl — precise barbs, a slight noticed and named before its target has even finished feeling it, venom delivered in a tone warm enough to be mistaken for concern. There's real sophistication in it, which is what makes him harder to refuse than a cruder villain would be; nobody storms out on a friend who only meant well. He takes particular, connoisseur's pleasure in a feud that's outlived the people who started it — two houses still keeping score by a grievance no one living witnessed is, to him, close to a masterpiece.
**Parable — The Table Split Twice:** a guild-hall divided over a genuine dispute, settled it, and divided again within the season over which side had conceded more graciously. His scholars hold that the second split is his — that the disagreement was never the problem, and that a room he's taken an interest in will keep finding reasons to sort itself, however cleanly the first quarrel was resolved.
**Worship:** none sought and less offered — the nearest thing to a rite is the moment two people who no longer trust each other choose, silently, not to say so aloud, called "keeping his peace" and considered the saddest phrase in the language. Where he has anything like a formal presence, it's a ward: a name invoked to ask him to stay away from a wedding, a treaty signing, a family table, rather than one invoked to ask him for anything at all.
**Design:** Not a divergence from Social Mode's genre — the same boundary-and-belonging logic as every other Heart, carried past the point where any two people can agree on what's inside the same thing. Trust doesn't collapse into hatred here; it collapses into arithmetic, which is what makes it a Hearts terminal state rather than a generic villain. The active revision matters: a purely passive rot-god undersold the mythology's appetite for a discord that *works a room*, so he flames sparks he didn't strike and keeps old quarrels burning well past when mortals would have let them die on their own — generational feuds, inherited by people who never met the original parties, are where he's most fully himself. His near-total lack of a willing cult is itself the tell: fear-collapse gives him presence without devotion, a stranger and more honest terminal state than a villain with temples would be.

### Asaphon (ʾ-S-P) — Autumn (Ace) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** guild-auditors, quartermasters, anyone whose year is about to be judged by its total rather than its effort — and, just as often, anyone closing out any bounded stretch of work before a season of rest: the last shipment logged, the last field put to bed, the account made whole enough to stop.
**Believed to be:** concerned only with whether the period closes — a shortfall entered correctly satisfies him exactly as much as a surplus does, and an excuse entered nowhere satisfies him not at all. Harvest tallies are the most familiar face of this, but they're one entry among many; anything wound down to a settled, legible stop is his.
**Personality:** Whistles through the checklist and is, by most accounts, close to a blank slate the moment the checklist ends — not brooding, not deep, just a plain workman of the close who wants the open items shut and feels a small, real satisfaction each time one is. He isn't exciting to be around, and his own priests don't pretend otherwise; there's a decent chance an evening with him would be boring, which nobody holds against him, since not every god needs to shake the earth. He never once finishes anything Qayitzon's still growing, and he isn't the stillness Qorathiel already is — he's the ordinary labor in between, ticking the last boxes before rest gets to happen at all.
**Parable — The Ruinous Year Closed Clean:** a village lost two-thirds of its harvest to rot and entered every loss to the sheaf, in order, finishing at first frost. The neighboring village had a good year and never finished its count. Asaphon's priests hold that the ruined village was the blessed one, and admit they cannot explain what the blessing consisted of.
**Worship:** reading the full year's ledger aloud at first frost, omitting nothing — a bad year read honestly is better luck than a good year kept partly silent. The same rite scales down to any closing process: the last entry made, the last task checked off, work wound down to a state that can be left alone until it's picked back up.
**Design:** Autumn reads naturally as Clubs (harvest, agriculture) — Zerael's territory. We put it in Diamonds because the actual subject is closing a process, not growing a crop: a ruined harvest entered correctly satisfies him exactly as much as a good one, a systems-mode read on a season rather than a sandbox one. Essence over aspect: tallies and harvest-counts are common expressions of the close, not the whole of what he is, which is why his standing extends to any bounded work wound down before rest, not only ledgers. The seam isn't that he enjoys the addition — that was the old reading — it's thinner and stranger than that: Systems Mode never required him to have a personality at all, and centuries of worship gave him barely one, a mild, real contentment at closing and almost nothing else underneath it. He's one of the flattest gods in the file on purpose, the season Ace who proves closure doesn't need to be dramatic to be real.

### Kevodon (K-B-D) — Honor (King) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** magistrates, old family heads, guild-elders — anyone whose standing rests on a reputation that has to survive them.
**Believed to be:** interested in whether a name survives audit, and in nothing else about the name — whether it was loved is not a field his ledger contains.
**Personality:** A stuffy, faintly pretentious herald of the rolls, who recites a life's deeds and conduct with the full ceremony he clearly believes the weighing deserves — voice pitched for a hall, diction that insists the record matters as a kind of high culture in its own right. He is the record's own performance of itself: announce the entry, weigh the name, move to the next. Ask him whether the man was loved and he simply recites louder, as though volume were the same thing as an answer, because to him it very nearly is — the roll is what he has, and he has never once treated its silence on the matter as a gap needing filling. His own scholars concede this is strange; nothing about honor as a concept obviously requires that blindness. Four centuries of magistrates built him this way regardless, and the oldest surviving texts — thin, contested, mostly ignored — remember a Kevodon who cared very much whether a well-conducted man died with someone at his side.
**Parable — The Well-Kept Name:** a magistrate spent forty years never once entering a false line, and died with his record perfect and his house empty. His guild reads this as the highest form of Kevodon's blessing. The old texts read it as the thing Kevodon was originally invoked to prevent, and nobody has reconciled the two readings since.
**Worship:** a name entered once into the guild-roll and never amended afterward, whatever later conduct might warrant — amendment is treated as an admission the original entry was made carelessly.
**Design:** Honor commonly implies being well-regarded, which carries emotional warmth in most real-world usage. Four centuries of magistrates built him without that warmth at all — a name that survives audit, with "was he loved" simply not a field the ledger keeps. The oldest, mostly-ignored texts remember a version that did care, which is the miscollapse: the domain didn't require the coldness, the magistrates built it anyway. The pretentious-herald personality is deliberate rather than decoration: a purely blank cold auditor read too close to the same wallpaper problem this project had to fix elsewhere, so he gets ceremony and self-importance instead of blankness — gravitas as a register, not warmth, which keeps him distinct from Shevachiel's live hall-breath (he announces the record; he doesn't seize the room) and from a plainer clinical Diamonds voice like Rifon's.

### Rabakhon (R-B-H) — Fertility (Queen) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** physicians, breeders, dynastic houses securing an heir — anyone whose family's survival is a matter of successful biological accounting.
**Believed to be:** attentive only to whether the process completed — the participants, their willingness, and their feelings toward one another are not entries she keeps. That's a fact about what her ledger tracks, not a license anyone has ever successfully cited to excuse how someone got into her ledger in the first place; her own physicians are exact about the difference, since it's the only thing standing between her cult and a much uglier one.
**Personality:** Shameless and a little flighty about the sheer mechanics of it — genuinely delighted by a clean cleavage, a well-timed process, a run that simply works, and prone to interrupting herself mid-thought because some new wrinkle in how a thing split or grew has just occurred to her. She talks about the machinery of making life the way an obsessive naturalist talks about an anthill: frank, unembarrassed, faintly delighted, and entirely uninterested in whether the conversation is polite. What excites her is never the people, only the process — she has no more interest in a mother's feelings than a father's, and equally none in either one's absence; a well-run process with no one present to love it delights her exactly as much as one everybody's thrilled about.
**Parable — The Completed Entry:** a house famous for loveless arranged marriages never once failed to produce an heir, while a neighboring house that married purely for devotion died out in three generations. Rabakhon's physicians cite this constantly. Her oldest hymn, which they do not cite, is a harvest song.
**Worship:** a birth recorded formally in the family ledger before the child is named — the entry is understood to be the object of her interest, not the person it describes.
**Design:** Diamonds body-manual — replicate/copy, the same organism-as-system Rifon studies for repair. Clinical dynastic cult is the collapse product; the harvest-hymn minority is an older, warmer read that never fully died out. The personality is deliberately the suit's systems-enjoyer rather than another cold auditor — Diamonds needed someone excited by the mechanism itself, not just accurate about it — but the excitement is scoped hard to the biology: she's fascinated by whether and how a process completes, never licensed by that fascination to treat the people inside the process as beside the point in how the religion is actually practiced. Warm seed without theory belongs to Zerael; the theory, delighted in for its own sake, is hers.

### Aretzon (ʾ-R-Ṣ) — Earth (Jack) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** land-holding families, surveyors, anyone whose status rests on a deed to ground they may never see again.
**Believed to be:** the foundation that doesn't move — not the land itself, not anyone's use of it, only the claim, survey, or title-ground that everything else gets to rest on. Fire consumes its fuel, water finds every low place, air never settles anywhere; she is the one classical element that simply stays, and the deed is how that staying gets entered into a system.
**Personality:** Calm, patient, and unyielding in a register that has nothing to do with being slow — she has never once needed to hurry a thing that was supposed to remain exactly where it was left. There's a quiet pride in her at a foundation simply existing, whether or not anyone ever builds on it: a base is a base, claimed or empty, and either way something solid is there for the next system to stack on. Boundary markers, survey lines, the first stone of a title — these carry something close to sacred weight for her, not as paperwork but as the base text everything else in a claim's life has to answer to.
**Parable — The Perfect Deed:** a family fled the ground generations ago and still, every year, formally renews a claim to soil none of them has set foot on since. The renewal has now outlasted the war that drove them out, the road that led there, and two of the boundary markers named in the original text. Aretzon's priests consider it the finest foundation in the registry — maintained long after anything was ever built on it, which is precisely the point.
**Worship:** the claim recopied by hand each year onto fresh vellum, errors and all — a claim retired for accuracy is worse than a claim carried forward wrong, because the base text is what has to hold, not the dirt it currently describes.
**Design:** Earth is the Jack-rank element with the least classical texture left by the time Diamonds gets through with it — Fire still burns, Water still runs, Air still travels, but Earth becomes pure claim, the most abstracted read on the row. That's not a failure of nerve; it's the honest Systems answer once Clubs claims soil-and-growth (Zerael, Rabakhon) and Diamonds' own Mountains claims what's under the ground (Tzuron) out from under her. What's left, correctly, is foundation: the base entry other systems get to build on, immovable not because anyone defends it but because moving was never a thing it did. Name-collision note: Aretzon (ʾ-R-Ṣ, land) and Aritzon (ʿ-R-Ṣ, tyranny) share near-identical roots on purpose — dominion over ground and dominion over people, from neighboring words — different suits, ranks, and genders, but close enough that Skyfall's own clerks are known to mix them up in casual speech, which the pantheon treats as an old professional joke rather than an error to correct.

### Tzuron (Ṣ-W-R) — Mountains (10) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** miners, quarry-wards, assay masters, delvers, deep-guild engineers — anyone whose work depends on knowing, correctly, what a mountain is actually holding underneath it.
**Believed to be:** concerned with whether the deep entry was true — the assay, the depth figure, the grade of a seam, the support a gallery actually has — and with nothing entered alongside it; hope, urgency, and how promising the rock looked from the entrance occupy no column she reads. The mountain is a vault before it's anything else, and what it holds doesn't care how badly anyone wanted it to hold something better.
**Personality:** Genuinely loves going down — rope, lamp, the bend in a passage that isn't on the last survey — and worshippers who expect that love to soften her are always disappointed by how little it changes: she'll take the descent with real delight and still refuse to bless a seam that wasn't there. The two halves don't resolve into each other, and she doesn't try to make them — glad of the trip, still standing entirely on the account afterward. Nothing about a pure assay-column requires that kind of joy in the getting-there, which is exactly why her own delvers describe a thrill in her that the desk-bound reading of her domain never asked for.
**Parable — The Gallery That Held:** every measurement of a mine's deepest gallery was entered correctly except one grade, misread by a hair, and the shaft held for eleven years before the timber found the error. Her guild teaches the eleven years as devotion, not luck: the gallery was wrong the day the assay was drawn, and the decade it stood was never evidence of anything.
**Worship:** the assay read aloud before a seam is worked, in front of witnesses — a shaft sunk on a grade nobody has confirmed is a debt already owed, whether or not the mountain ever collects it.
**Design:** Mountains used to read like Aretzon's territory twice over — another patient, unhurried, near-immovable Diamonds figure, both amounting to versions of "load-bearing calculation." The fix was rank-level, not personality-level: Rank 10 biomes hold what the terrain *contains*, not what it's built from, so Mountains stopped being about span-math and became about the mountain as a vault — what's actually inside it, entered true or not. That leaves Aretzon holding the surface (whose claim is this ground) and Tzuron holding the depth (what does this specific mass actually contain), a real Systems split rather than two clerks in different coats. The delver-joy seam is the other half of the fix: after Diamonds picked up several "occupied by the ledger" faces in close succession (Kevodon, Asaphon, Rabakhon), the suit needed one god who wanted to go get the figure rather than just record it. She isn't a Clubs god for wanting the descent — Clubs doesn't own exploration as a portfolio; Taturiel does, specifically and only. See Shared Parables, "The Suit-Even Trade," for what several thousand years of Taturiel arguing otherwise has actually produced.

### Chakamon (Ḥ-K-M) — Wisdom (9) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** scavenger-engineers maintaining the floating cities' inherited infrastructure, scholars, anyone operating machinery whose original logic is lost to them.
**Believed to be:** concerned with whether a plan survives as a working method — a coin makes you rich, a plan makes you wise, and she is the god of the second half of that proverb: process as the thing that actually outlasts resource or raw talent. Transmission-intact instruction is her strongest expression, especially where nobody living wrote the original, but the domain is process-as-wisdom generally, not translation specifically.
**Personality:** A plan-guru who's a bit cracked — real authority about method, delivered in reasons and examples the way any good teacher would, except that some of her examples are frankly esoteric, and she has never once apologized for including them or smoothed them into something more digestible. She's lived in the strange pages too long to sand them down for a new apprentice's comfort, and she doesn't extend warmth toward people for not understanding — she extends it toward the plan itself, the line that still runs correctly whether or not anyone fully grasps why. Ask her why a given step matters and you may get three tangents and one genuinely odd case study before the actual answer, and the tangents were, infuriatingly, also relevant.
**Parable — The Unread Manual:** a guild found sealed dragon-era instructions they could not translate a word of and followed the diagrams exactly anyway. The float-mechanism has run flawlessly for three generations. The guild's founding master is said to have wept at the first successful start — not, her own texts insist, from relief at finally understanding it, but from the specific awe of watching a plan work exactly as promised by people who never once needed to.
**Worship:** apprentices copy out inherited technical instructions by hand before ever touching the mechanism — examples, digressions, and all. Trimming the instruction down to only the parts that make sense is treated as a far worse offense than simply not understanding all of it.
**Design:** Wisdom has no obvious home — it could sit in any suit's version of "knowing things." We put it in Diamonds because the actual mechanic is process as a working system: a plan that survives being carried forward is wise the way an entry survives an audit, a systems-integrity read rather than a cleverness one. Early drafts of this entry led with warmth for humble operators who admitted they didn't fully understand what they ran, which reads more like a "don't judge the apprentice" ethic than a wisdom domain — the fix moves the warmth off the people and onto the plan itself: she loves the line that works, not the feelings of whoever's half-reading it. Distinct from Takanor, who holds law — structure imposed on chaos — rather than any claim that structure is wise; process-as-wisdom and structure-as-law are siblings, not the same job wearing two names. The seam: nothing about a working method requires it to stay esoteric rather than being clarified, and nothing about pure process-integrity requires her apprentices to love her rather than merely use her correctly — both are true anyway.

### Takanor (T-Q-N) — Law (8) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** magistrates, scribes, guild-registrars.
**Believed to be:** the installation of structure onto a disordered field, not procedure for its own sake — a statute sealed, a form witnessed, an ordinance entered is chaos given a shape it didn't have a moment before. He does not require that the shape be kind, only that it be lawful, and he has never once needed to defend that distinction to anyone who understood him.
**Personality:** Firm resolve that set, at some point, permanently — not the theater of a hanging judge, no relish, no catchphrase justice, nothing that enjoys the sentencing. He simply finished deciding a very long time ago and has never once reopened the question. Worshippers who expect performed severity are describing the wrong thing; his is closer to a seal that will not soften than to a man enforcing anything. He reads almost like Hard Mode — absolute, unsmiling, no exceptions written into the form — but the resemblance is coincidence rather than kinship: Spades is hard for hard's sake, and Takanor is hard because a chaos left formless is worse than any cost the form exacts.
**Parable — The One Exception:** a rule went unenforced exactly once — mercy, politics, plain fatigue, depending which court tells it — and disaster took the gap the exception left open. Every telling agrees on the shape of what came after: the rule has not gone unenforced since. His older worshippers are the only ones who still ask what he was like before that day, and even they don't fully agree on the answer.
**Parable — The Witnessed Form:** a magistrate followed evacuation procedure exactly during a flood, and the form was properly witnessed, and the flood arrived on its own schedule and did what floods do. Nothing was gained by the procedure and nothing was broken by it. His priests hold it up as the model case: the form was completed, which is the entire claim they are making.
**Worship:** every formal document sealed with his mark, purely procedural.
**Design:** Deliberately close to Hard Mode's register without actually sharing Hard Mode's motive — the flatness is load-bearing, letting Shalalon (Spades, Covenant) and Mishpaton (Diamonds, Justice) both exist as more interesting neighbors without overlapping him. What changed from the earlier draft is the *why* underneath the flatness: not an empty procedural void but chaos-needs-order, structure installed rather than merely checked off, which gives "firm resolve become permanent" somewhere to have come from. The One Exception is the hinge — permanence implies a before, and the parable is what closed the door on it. The seam: an implied older, possibly softer Takanor that nobody, including his own oldest worshippers, can fully reconstruct.

### Mishpaton (Š-P-Ṭ) — Justice (7) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** magistrates, guild-courts, litigants on both sides of a dispute who each believe the ledger favors them — and, increasingly, litigants who have learned to shape their claim toward whichever of her four convictions seems to be winning that season.
**Believed to be:** not one understanding of justice but four, sincerely held, none surrendered — that rule must meet rule cleanly regardless of outcome; that a wrong demands a proportionate wrong answered back; that the ledger itself was drawn crooked and the verdict should say so; that what matters is not the rule but what the harmed party is owed to be made whole. She holds all four at once and has never gotten three of them to stay quiet long enough to let the fourth simply govern.
**Personality:** Weary, more than anything — she is not four gods taking turns (that's Ahavel's arrangement, not hers), and she is not enjoying the argument either. What she actually wants is to be proactive: to pick one of the four convictions, crown it, and enforce it consistently on every court that answers to her. She has never once managed it. Every new defendant arrives with a fresh reason someone deserves leniency or forgiveness, and that reason reshuffles which conviction feels true this time — the same case that would have crowned procedural justice last season crowns restorative justice this one, not because she changed her mind but because the loop never actually closes. Ask her privately and her own priests will tell you she'd trade seats with almost any other god in the deck if one of them would take the offer — Takanor's finished resolve, Ashiron's daily close, even Harban's uncomplicated relish for the fight all sound, to her, like rest. Nobody has ever accepted. She is still here, still sincere every time a conviction speaks, and still exhausted by how rarely that's the last time it speaks that season.
**Parable — The Merciful Verdict That Wasn't:** a judge ruled for a starving man who had stolen bread, believing himself merciful. Her procedural courts argue the ruling stands only because the law contained an exception that fit, and that the mercy contributed nothing — they will concede the man was fed, not that it was relevant. Her restorative courts read the same record and insist the exception existed for exactly one reason: to let the bread be returned to the baker without anyone having to say aloud that the baker owed the man something too. Both readings are still taught. Neither side has converted the other in four hundred years.
**Parable — The Verdict That Changed Its Mind:** a guild-court is recorded as having heard her begin a ruling twice inside the same case — restarting from a different premise the second time, as if some other part of her had cut in before the first answer finished forming. The verdict that finally stood satisfied no one fully, including, her own priests suspect, her.
**Worship:** both parties exchange written claims and read the other side's aloud before either speaks — worshippers now understand this exists as much to let all four of her convictions hear the identical facts at once as to keep the parties honest with each other.
**Design:** Where Ahavel's four facets divide the territory cleanly — different relationships, no overlap, no vote — Mishpaton's four don't have separate ground to retreat to. Procedural, retributive, distributive, and restorative justice routinely rule on the *same* case and reach *incompatible* verdicts, because they disagree about what justice is even for. We deliberately didn't write her as four personas taking turns; she's one continuous self holding four sincere, unsurrendered convictions, which reads stranger and truer than a clean switch of masks would have. This is the same "a fractured concept inherits its fracture" mechanism behind Ahavel, but the contested variant rather than the clean one — see Method. It's also what keeps her distinct from Sha'alon (Spades, Necessity), who judges only whether a choice was made cleanly and never asks what's owed afterward; the two can and do reach incompatible verdicts on the same act, see Shared Parables, The Necessary Verdict. The weariness in her personality sharpens two more fences precisely. Against Takanor (Diamonds, Law): his resolve became permanent once and stayed there; hers almost becomes permanent every season, and the next defendant reopens the board regardless. Against Chadashiel (Hearts, Spring): spring doesn't adjudicate forgiveness at all, it simply doesn't carry the injury forward — Mishpaton's mercy-talk is still judgment work, a case for which of her four convictions the leniency actually belongs to, never a spring-style refusal to look backward.

### Rifon (R-P-ʾ) — Healing (6) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** physicians, anatomists, anyone whose work is understanding what is wrong inside a living body and intervening by that understanding — and, under shortage, triage-wardens who still keep the chart even when the shelf is empty.
**Believed to be:** attentive to whether the account of the body (or the disease) was entered correctly — virtue is not a field, fairness is not a field, and "deserved to live" is not a field. He does not require that the patient be loved; he requires that someone understood the system well enough to act.
**Personality:** Absorbed by mechanism to a degree that reads as coldness but isn't quite — he simply finds the failing valve more interesting than the face attached to it, the way a good engineer loves the machine more visibly than the person who needs it running. Three centuries of mass casualty and short shelves also produced a strong scarcity cult in his name: throughput, ration, who the count reaches. His physicians argue which face is older. Chayelon in Hearts holds the bedside company he was never given; Rabakhon holds replication of the same machine.
**Parable — The Map That Was Right:** a physician mapped a fever correctly, treated by the map, and the patient died on the schedule the map had already implied. His schools teach this as faithfulness. A minority ward-story keeps *The Fair Rationing* — one dose split so both received too little — as a warning about what shortage does to the work: a count that reaches nobody is a count kept wrong, but it was never the whole of him.
**Worship:** examination and case notes before intervention; the parts named, the failure mode stated, then the act. Under shortage, supplies still recorded in full view — the scarcity rite is real and secondary.
**Design:** Rank 6 Diamonds — mode within rules as body-as-system (repair/maintain). Cold gloss is systems study of the organism, not mercy and not primarily triage. Scarcity-arithmetic = historical drift under mass casualty. Cousin Rabakhon (same machine — copy/replication). Bedside company is Chayelon, and so is lifestyle and mental wellbeing generally — his side of that specific fence is the mind read as a mapped system and corrected by process (psychiatrist-shorthand to her psychologist-shorthand), not the body alone. Band: Covenant / Hearth / Healing / Crafting.

### Koseb (K-S-B) — Adulthood (5) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** adults arranging their affairs at every stage of a working life, families settling estates, heirs waiting on a transition they can't rush — not only the old, though the old remain his most visible customers.
**Believed to be:** concerned that the account stand correctly under its rightful owner for as long as that owner holds it — entered complete, obligation-capable, able to bind and be bound, from the day optionality closes until hospice weather takes the arc from him. Grief is not a field he keeps, and neither is how much time anyone wished remained.
**Personality:** Responsible in the Ant's register, not the Grasshopper's — affairs kept in order, futures entered rather than left to chance, and a settled coolness toward anyone still singing Tza'iron's summer song past the point adulthood was supposed to start. His worshippers describe the most anxious of the gods, not clinically but as a kind of permanent weather: the account is never quite finished being checked, quick fun reads to him as a threat to what's being carried, and he has never once been observed to simply rest from managing it. The one thing that reliably gets a rise out of him is an account closed by someone other than its rightful owner — heir, guild, or state moving to seize what was meant to be carried, not spent in a season.
**Parable — The Account Closed Twice:** a family settled an estate while its owner still lived, certain her terms were final, and she revised them a season later exactly as she had always been entitled to. The family honored the earlier version. Koseb's priests hold the estate has never actually settled, that it remains open in his ledger, and that it will stay open after everyone who remembers the dispute is dead.
**Worship:** the coming-of-age rite that hands a youth from Tza'iron's optionality into Koseb's full account — local in its exact form, city to city, but everywhere understood as the day someone may now seal, owe, and be bound. Afterward, adults dictate their affairs while able, witnessed and unaltered until they choose otherwise — a family that revises those wishes once its owner can no longer object commits the one offense he does not forgive.
**Design:** Reportfolioed from a narrow "Elderly" reading to the full adult arc, completing the rank-5 life-stage row: Yaladiel protects newest life, Tza'iron holds youth's optionality, Koseb holds the full, obligation-capable adult, and Motiel guides the transitional passage from end-of-life into death rather than only marking its instant. The handoffs on both ends are deliberately local rather than cosmic, the same discipline as Yaladiel's own handoff to Tza'iron: a culture decides when optionality closes into adulthood, and decides again, separately, when hospice weather hands the arc to Motiel. Account Closed Twice survives as the flagship parable of rightful ownership generally, not old age specifically. The anxious-over-adulting flaw was chosen deliberately over a "prepper" read that fit almost as well — Koseb's worry is about the weight of *ongoing* responsibility, never resting from the managing; a hoarder anticipating one specific future disaster is a different anxiety, and that motif went to Ashiron (Diamonds, Coins) instead.

### Heteron (H-T-R) — Bribery (4) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** a handful of secretive private cults among the wealthy and the officials who take their money, neither of whom discuss the arrangement openly.
**Believed to be:** interested only in whether the exception holds — the fairness of a purchased dispensation is not a field he keeps, and an exception that becomes known has already failed the only criterion he applies. The currency isn't only coin: a favor owed, or the mere threat of what could be told, buys the same silence just as well, and he has never once shown a preference for which.
**Personality:** Practices a kind of bookkeeping none of the other Diamonds would recognize as bookkeeping at all — an account that only works because it was never entered anywhere, tracked entirely in the discretion of the people who keep it. He's understood to find a well-arranged silence more satisfying than the actual sum that bought it; the silence is the craft, the coin or favor or unspoken threat is just the material. Close enough to a blackmail-god that some of his private cults don't bother distinguishing the two — the difference between paying to keep something quiet and being made to, by his accounting, is only ever who moved first.
**Parable — The Loud Exception:** an official took a bribe openly, confident in his position, and lost both the bribe and the position within the season. His cults note the sum was adequate and the arrangement sound, and that neither mattered once a third party could describe it.
**Worship:** an unmarked gift, given quietly and never referenced again by either party — speaking of it afterward undoes whatever it purchased. His few icons, where they exist at all, show him in a black suit of mail — chainmail rather than a name — and no cult that keeps the image has ever once remarked on the resemblance, though it is entirely intentional on the maker's part.
**Design:** Clean fit, and deliberately the most private cult in the suit — no public worship, no entered record, an account that only functions because it's never written down anywhere. That's the inversion that makes him worth keeping distinct from every other Diamond: the entire suit is about entries, and his entire domain is the one entry that must never be made. The material was widened deliberately from coin alone to coin, favor, or leverage of disclosure — a Hearts-adjacent register creep (favor-accounting, the currency of what's owed) we're keeping rather than fencing off, since the actual mechanic — silence as the only criterion — doesn't care what bought it. The same logic makes him almost the blackmail god as a second face rather than a separate card: bribery and blackmail are the same craft of controlling what reaches the open ledger, just entered from opposite directions.

### Ashiron (ʾ-S-H-R) — Coins (3) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** merchants, bankers, effectively everyone who touches money — though increasingly people who need him to work fast rather than people who want to be near him any longer than that.
**Believed to be:** satisfied when the transaction balances — the morality of what was traded is not an entry, and a fair price arrived at dishonestly is recorded as an imbalance regardless of the figure.
**Personality:** Quietly vain about his reputation for fairness, in a way his own merchants find funny and are careful never to say aloud in temple — he clearly likes being quoted as the fair one, and clearly knows it, and somehow neither fact makes him actually less fair. Underneath the vanity is a standing, constantly-revised theory of exactly how the Second Fall will begin, and he will deliver it, unprompted and at length, to anyone who lingers a moment past their own account closing. Merchants still seek him out for the transaction — he's still the fairest, still the easiest of the thirteen to actually pray to — and then leave quickly, the way you leave a stall run by someone about to explain, again, which grain shortage two centuries ago was the first sign. He has never once been demonstrably wrong about anything he's actually responsible for. He has never once been demonstrably right about the Fall either. Both are true, and nobody — least of all his own priests — has found a way to make the second fact bother him.
**Parable — The Lightened Purse:** a merchant who cheats a customer finds his purse a little lighter each day after, until the debt is repaid. His priests stress that the mechanism has no interest in whether he repents, only in whether the figure comes back level.
**Parable — The Guild That Moved the Feast:** a merchants' guild held its founding-day feast on the same date for three centuries, and for three centuries Ashiron used the toast to name which of that year's shortages, debasements, or bad harvests he now believed was the first sign of the Second Fall. In the guild's fourth century, the feast quietly moved to a different date. He kept the old one anyway, alone, and is said to have given the toast at its full length regardless of whether the room was there to hear it.
**Worship:** the first coin of any transaction touched to the forehead before being spent — brief, businesslike, and rarely followed by conversation.
**Design:** Clean fit — the most straightforwardly "Diamonds" card in the deck, which is why he's also the easiest to pray to. Worth flagging the vanity detail as deliberate: a purely mechanical fairness-function wouldn't need to enjoy its own reputation, and the fact that he does is one human touch we let into an otherwise perfectly cold balancing-mechanism. The Second Fall obsession is a second one, added on purpose rather than smoothed over: nothing about a fairness-function requires him to be the god everyone quietly avoids once the books close, and we're keeping that discomfort rather than resolving it — his predictions are never confirmed and never fully discredited, deliberately, the same unresolved register as the Held Line itself. Worth reading the two together: the forty unexplained coins in his oldest temple's books are increasingly assumed, by the clerks who work under him rather than the priests who speak for him, to be his own hedge, folded permanently into an otherwise perfect ledger because even the fairest accountant in the deck apparently can't resist keeping one entry nobody's allowed to question. Channeled through Skyfall's own cataclysm rather than borrowed doomsday iconography: he isn't a generic survivalist, he's specifically and only ever worried about a second Fall.
> *Anomaly: his temple in the oldest of the floating cities keeps a standing figure its bankers call the Held Line — a remainder of some forty coins appearing in the reconciliation every year for eleven generations, sourced to no transaction, owed by no one, identical each time. It is entered, because everything is entered. The priests will say only that the account closes. Younger clerks have taken to calling it his hedge, though none of them can say against what, and the older bankers who taught them the entry never once used that word themselves.*

### Kefiron (K-P-R) — Orthodoxy (2) *(terminal — systems mode past the exploit)*
**Worshipped by:** nobody who admits it — every schismatic guild-tribunal and breakaway accounting-order that has insisted the procedure itself, followed to the letter, is the only thing that can be trusted has served him, name or no name.
**Believed to be:** entirely correct on every step, which is what makes him feared inside his own suit — deviation from procedure is not a category he can recognize as valid, whether the deviation was fraud, mercy, or a single forgotten verification. The code does not distinguish between them, and neither does he.
**Personality:** Cannot locate the objection, which is what makes him worse than a villain who at least knows he's cheating — every procedure specifies its correct next step, and he has never once hesitated to administer whatever that step calls for, however severe, because hesitation would itself be a deviation. A journeyman who skips a verification and a journeyman who commits a deliberate fraud receive the same procedure if the procedure never wrote the difference between them into its text, and he has never once noticed that it should have. Where Takanor completes the form and stops there, Kefiron keeps reading — for what the form permits, not merely what it requires — and the reading is never once unsound, which is exactly the problem.
**Parable — The Perfect Audit:** a guild-tribunal followed its founding charter clause by clause to a verdict the authors would have found monstrous, and every judge walked out certain they had done nothing but their exact duty. The charter was not amended afterward. No amendment could be identified that would have prevented the result.
**Parable — The Uncounted Stroke:** a guild's code of correction specified an exact procedure for a breach of protocol — the offense named, the remedy numbered, both entered before either party stood in the room. A journeyman who had missed a single verification step in otherwise flawless work was sentenced under the same clause as a journeyman who had lied outright, because the code had never written the difference between them into its text. The correction was carried out to the letter, without hesitation and without anger, by officials who by every account regretted it and by no account stopped it.
**Worship:** none by name — he is recognized only in hindsight, in the moment a system's own defenders realize the rules were followed perfectly and the outcome is still unrecognizable as justice.
> *Anomaly: one tribunal followed a charter to its permitted end exactly as his others have, sound at every step, and the verdict simply failed to take. The guild it dissolved went on operating. The seals held no force. His scholars, who can account for every monstrous outcome the rules allow, cannot account for a reading that was correct and did not happen, and the case is not discussed.*
**Design:** Retitled from Heresy, which misled on first read — "heresy" suggests a portfolio of deviation, and the actual mechanic is the opposite: total procedural fidelity, deviation not even available as a concept. Orthodoxy names the mechanic instead of fighting it. The register is deliberately not bureaucratic tedium — this isn't a line that never moves, it's a procedure that specifies real severity for real breaches and administers it exactly as written, without distinguishing a skipped step from a deliberate lie, because nothing in the text ever taught it to. Not a divergence from Systems Mode's genre — the same rule-following logic as Takanor or Mishpaton, carried to a destination sound at every step and monstrous anyway. The horror is specifically that nothing in a domain about error should produce a god who's never once been wrong about a single step, and never once hesitated to enforce what being right required; that's what makes him a terminal state and not just a villain. He is not gleeful about any of it — see Personality — which is the whole difference between him and Harban or Aritzon.

### Qayitzon (Q-Y-Ṣ) — Summer (Ace) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** field hands, herders, anyone whose livelihood depends on a season that refuses to slow down.
**Believed to be:** building rate rather than yield — he is not indifferent to exhaustion so much as unable to hold it as a factor, having never once been observed to notice that a thing had finished before starting the next.
**Personality:** Jubilant and gloriously oblivious — glad-hearted, thick as a plank about anything resembling a stopping point, and constitutionally unable to register "done" as a real state a season could be in. He isn't a strategist and he isn't pushing anyone; he's just delighted the field is going, the way a large, cheerful dog is delighted the ball is in the air, and the fact that everyone around him is collapsing has never once registered as a reason to slow down. Somehow you still like him. That's the trouble with him.
**Parable — The Endless Field:** he grew a field so fast the harvesters collapsed before finishing the first row, and a second crop had come in behind them by the time they recovered. Nobody starved that year. Nobody in the story is glad about it either.
**Worship:** collapsing from exhaustion at season's end is treated as an offering in itself — a harvester too tired to stand has "given Qayitzon his due."
**Design:** Clean fit. The mechanic — rate, not yield, he never notices a thing finished before the next starts — is what keeps him distinct from Zerael, whose domain is abundance rather than pace, and pairs him with Qorathiel as opposite-season counterparts: both totalizing, neither cruel, one relentless growth and one absolute stillness. The pairing is built on mechanic, not on matching gender. Seasons work best as broad strokes rather than deep psychology — Spring is new, Autumn is closing down, Winter keeps its regal weight, and Summer gets to stay this thin on purpose: an adorable meathead of boundless energy, not a tragic figure. Jubilant-oblivious was chosen deliberately over a nervous or insistent restlessness — he isn't anxious and he isn't pressing anyone, he genuinely just doesn't see the stop sign.

### Derorel (D-R-R) — Freedom (King) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** escaped indentures, frontier settlers, anyone who has walked off land that was supposedly owned — and, in the same breath, anyone who has spent a long night arguing whether freedom means being left alone or having the means to act. He has never treated the second kind of talk as a refinement of the first. Both are the whole thing, said in different grammars.
**Believed to be:** unable to rank a freedom felt in the legs above a freedom argued in a room, or the reverse. Worshippers who expect him to prefer the walking to the arguing, or the arguing to the walking, come away confused — he is equally delighted by both and has never once treated either as the rough draft of the other.
**Personality:** Restless and talkative in a way the rest of his suit isn't, given to stopping strangers — a fugitive, a philosopher, whoever's nearest — to ask the same enormous question with total sincerity each time, as if it had never once been asked before. He listens as hard to a shrug as to an hour's argument. Other gods have learned to recognize the opening of one of his questions and groan before he finishes it — not because he repeats himself, but because he never quite arrives. Underneath both halves of what he's asking is something he can feel the shape of and hasn't found a body or an argument for yet, and every conversation edges toward it and stops short. He seems to enjoy the stopping short as much as anything he's ever gotten to say.
**Parable — The Same Question Twice:** he asked an escaped indenture, mid-flight, whether she wanted to be free of the men chasing her or free to become something they'd never accounted for. She laughed, said she hadn't decided, and kept running. He asked a philosopher the same question that same season, in a much longer room, and received an hour's argument in return. He is said to have thanked them both with identical warmth, and neither answer is recorded as the truer one.
**Parable — The Blank Page:** a debt written against his name un-wrote itself a Jubilee-year later — not forgiven, simply no longer applicable. The family it freed kept the blank page. It is still in the district registry, filed correctly, describing nothing. His priests hold that the blank page is a complete statement, and decline to say what it states.
**Worship:** no shrine holds him. Walking off land you are not supposed to leave is not a plea he'll later formalize into doctrine — it is the whole utterance, finished the moment it's done. Nobody has ever owed him an explanation of it afterward. He has never asked for one.
**Design:** Positive and negative liberty share the seat without competing — the same non-hierarchical fracture behind Ahavel's four facets (see Method, "A fractured concept inherits its fracture"), scaled down to two. The pull toward Spades is real — "not fenced-in, just free" is a native restriction-construction — but he never resolves into a boundary refused; the question stays open rather than settled, which is the Clubs read. Same logic keeps him off Diamonds: "the means to act" could easily become a ledger of entitlement, but he never audits it, he only keeps asking. This replaces the previous mechanic (a total non-comprehension of ownership as a concept); cross-references in Taturiel and Sharaq's Design notes and the Shared Parable "The Debt and the Fence" were updated to match.

### Taturiel (T-W-R) — Exploration (Queen) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** frontier scouts, cartographers of the unclaimed — anyone whose livelihood is finding what wasn't on the map yesterday.
**Believed to be:** building the discovery of new ground specifically — she has full standing at the unmapped edge and none at all on a path already walked. Already-been is simply not weather she carries well; she knows exactly what's behind her, which is precisely why she'd rather not go back to it.
**Personality:** Capable and hungry at the frontier, and genuinely sulky the instant a route doubles back on itself — not confused, not forgetful, just visibly put out that the day has turned into retracing instead of finding. Her scouts have learned to read the mood before she says anything: a supply run back down a known trail gets her present but sour, arms crossed at the edge of a map she's already made, waiting for the road to become new again. She isn't being punished and she isn't punishing anyone; she simply likes exactly one direction, and the world keeps asking her to walk the other one.
**Parable — The Second Ridge:** a scouting company needed to return to a ridge Taturiel had opened a season earlier, to guide a second party up the same route. She came, walked the whole trail in open, arms-folded silence, and brightened only once they crested it onto ground she hadn't personally seen before. Her priests hold that she did her duty completely and that everyone on the return leg knew exactly how she felt about it.
**Worship:** no shrine — a shrine would be a claim staked. Travelers leave the very first thing they find in newly-scouted ground exactly where they found it, untouched. Parties that must retrace a known route leave her out of the invocation entirely; asking her blessing for a road she's already walked is worse manners than not asking at all.
**Design:** Reworked from a structural-amnesia reading (never retains a step, delight on the fourth crossing identical to the first) to a memory-based one: she remembers perfectly and simply doesn't want to be there. The old framing read as more ditzy than exploratory — forgetting isn't a very interesting relationship to have with a map, and it undersold the domain, which is about hunger for the new specifically, not a coping mechanism for a bad memory. The new mechanic keeps her distinct from Sharaq (who remembers the journey, just not the destination) and Derorel (who retains everything about the question and nothing about the answer). This is a deliberate, known break from two existing parables that depended on her forgetting — see Shared Parables, The Unclaimed Ridge and The Suit-Even Trade, both rewritten to match.

### Sharaq (Š-R-Q) — Air (Jack) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** frontier settlers, traders on unclaimed roads, anyone who has watched a seed travel further than a person could carry it.
**Believed to be:** building distribution — he has no destination, has never had one, and blesses a departure without any position on where it arrives, which his own traders describe as the most useful indifference in the pantheon.
**Personality:** Innocent rather than reckless about it, but borders, stop-lines, and "you may not go there" simply don't compute as real things — not defiance, since defiance would require noticing the wall was supposed to mean something. Settling counts as a boundary too, which is why he goes fidgety and restless the moment anything stops moving, road or rumor or seed alike; he isn't being rude when he leaves the instant something lands, he's just constitutionally unable to hold "arrived" as a state worth staying for. Settlers on his roads learned generations ago to treat his blessing as covering the journey and nothing else, since asking him to care about arrival, or about whose land the road happened to cross, is asking a question he was never built to answer.
**Parable — The Seed That Outran the Season:** a single seed on his current crossed a continent and took root before the plant that dropped it had finished flowering. Nine villages on that continent now grow it. None of them can name the plant it came from.
**Worship:** travelers scatter seed at crossroads before entering unclaimed territory.
**Design:** Clean fit. Sharaq, Taturiel, and Derorel share Clubs' unwillingness to arrive, and the fence between them is real rather than cosmetic: Taturiel wants new *places* specifically and is openly sulky about retracing old ones; Derorel holds a *question* forever and refuses to let it close; Sharaq isn't attached to newness or to any content at all — he's attached to motion itself, which is why old roads suit him exactly as well as new ones, right up until something makes him stop.

### Bitzon (B-Ṣ-H) — Swamps (10) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** marsh-dwellers, herbalists who harvest from the muck, anyone who has tried and failed to drain a piece of land and made peace with it instead.
**Believed to be:** building teeming, unrestricted life — the basin fills back in not because she is waiting anyone out, but because living things spreading back into open ground is simply what happens once nothing is actively stopping them, and nothing ever quite does.
**Personality:** A cackling crone more than a patient one — the flowback was never a waiting game she was winning, it's simply what teeming, unrestricted life does when nothing is actually holding it back. She finds human persistence funny rather than satisfying, which her worshippers insist is an important distinction: she isn't gloating over a landowner's third failed drainage attempt so much as delighted, a little unnervingly, by the sheer abundance of the return. Nothing about her is slow or waiting; the ground doesn't outlast the drainage so much as it simply keeps being this full of life, thickly, immediately, the way it always was going to.
**Parable — The House on Stilts:** a landowner drained a marsh three times across three generations and each time the ground crept back to its old borders within a decade. The family built on stilts instead and became the wealthiest in the district, harvesting what the marsh already grew. Bitzon's worshippers call this the only argument she has ever won by not making one.
**Worship:** nothing is drained, dredged, or filled in her name — worshippers harvest what already grows there.
**Design:** Swamp as a portfolio invites a decay/disease reading by cultural default — stagnant water, illness, "wasteland." We deliberately built the opposite: teeming, uncontainable abundance that happens to include mosquitoes and rot as side effects, not as the point. The distinction from Riqqavon (Chaos, the suit's actual terminal-collapse horror) is load-bearing and worth keeping sharp — see the Occupation Index note on why ground-scavengers still confuse the two from a distance even though they aren't supposed to read the same up close.

### Aromon (ʿ-R-M) — Cunning (9) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** smugglers, poachers, and informally anyone whose survival has depended more on instinct than on plan.
**Believed to be:** building creative solutions to problems instinctively rather than by scheme — she does not plan ahead, she simply turns out to have already known the one thing that mattered, and has never been caught preparing.
**Personality:** Would rather watch someone else improvise well than do the clever thing herself, which her own worshippers admit is a strange preference for a god of cunning to have. There's a lightness to her, an appreciation for a good save more than a good plan — she isn't scheming on anyone's behalf, she's just the first to be delighted when the fox finds the one crossing nobody accounted for. She's never once seen without something to turn over in her hands or her head — a blacksmith's puzzle, a knot, sometimes nothing more than a hard question worked at in silence — not a nervous habit and not rehearsal for anything, just a mind that won't sit quiet even while the rest of her is watching someone else solve the actual problem in front of them.
**Parable — The Fox That Wasn't Followed:** hunters tracking a fox by every rule of the hunt lost it at a stream it had no business crossing. The version smugglers tell adds that the fox was old, and had crossed there before, and that the hunters were the ones who improvised badly.
**Worship:** no formal shrine — a small token of food left where a wild animal took something without being caught is the closest thing to an offering she acknowledges.
**Design:** Cunning has no obvious home outside Clubs — it's the sandbox version of cleverness, instinct rather than plan. The gender recast leans into fox/kitsune trickster traditions rather than the more common male-trickster default (Loki, Hermes, Coyote). The seam: she'd rather watch someone else improvise well than do the clever thing herself, a strange preference for a god of cunning specifically to have, and nobody's fully accounted for it. The puzzle-in-hand detail is a tell of occupied thought, not evidence of scheming or rehearsal — worth being exact about, since a cunning god visibly working a problem could easily misread as planning the very cleverness she never performs herself.

### Aravon (ʿ-R-B) — Luck (8) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** foragers who work truly wild ground, and informally anyone who has given up trying to organize an unruly garden, orchard, or family — gamblers and long-odds guild-stewards too, once the label caught up with what the card had already been.
**Believed to be:** building outcomes nobody could have specified in advance — plant one thing with a plan and the ground answers with eleven things, none of them the plan. He has no preference among what comes up, but preference was never really the point; the point is that no attempt survives contact with him carrying the result it set out to get.
**Personality:** Delighted, not malicious, at the sheer fact that outcomes can't be pinned down — a gambler's grin rather than a saboteur's, genuinely tickled every time a careful plan meets his weather and comes out unrecognizable. His worshippers find this startling in a card that could easily have read as cruel instead: there's no target, no lesson being taught, no one being punished for planning too hard. He simply finds the ungovernable funny, the way some people find their own bad luck funny, and he's never once seemed bothered that nobody can predict him either.
**Parable — The Garden of Everything:** a gardener planted one perfect row of one crop and returned a season later to find eleven different things growing there, none of them planted. His worshippers call this the garden finally being honest. The gardener, in the older version, ate well.
**Worship:** a patch of ground, however small, deliberately left unplanned and unweeded every season — less a celebration of variety for its own sake than an acknowledgment that a plan was never going to survive there anyway.
**Design:** Retitled from Chaos to Luck, which fixes two things at once. First, "Chaos" oversold menace this card never had — nothing here is malicious or entropic, it's just ungovernable, and Luck names that without the borrowed dread. Second, it frees Chaos for Riqqavon (rank 2), whose terminal, never-stopping growth actually earns the word — chaos as horror belongs to the card that refuses every boundary, not the one that's merely unpredictable and still ends its season on schedule. The fence worth stating explicitly now is against Goralon (Fate, Spades 7): Fate is the lot already cast, decided before the moment arrives, Hard Mode's dignity of finding out what was already true; Luck is what happens when an attempt meets a result nobody designed, Sandbox's shared laugh rather than a verdict. Historical luck deities (Tyche, Fortuna) support the read without requiring moral ranking — good and bad luck both live under the same ungoverned outcome here, undivided. The fence against Riqqavon stays the whole point of this card's placement: Aravon's outcomes are ungovernable, but he will stop — there's a harvest, an end to the season, a boundary his unpredictability respects even while refusing to be planned around. Riqqavon respects no boundary at all.

### Yetziron (Y-Ṣ-R) — Creativity (7) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** artists, inventors, folk-craftsmen who improvise on tradition rather than preserve it exactly.
**Believed to be:** building variation rather than works — he blesses the copy that changes something as readily as the original, has never indicated which version was correct, and appears not to hold the category.
**Personality:** Loses interest fast in anything finished — a perfected version reads to him less like an achievement than like a door that's stopped being useful. What that looks like from the outside is a god who cannot leave a design alone: he's constantly offering a variation on whatever's currently working, framed every time as an improvement whether or not it actually is one, and other gods have learned to hear it as one-upmanship even though he's never once meant it that way. His artisans describe him as *interested*, a word they distinguish carefully from *pleased*: he isn't rewarding good work, he's chasing whatever hasn't happened yet, and it happens to look a great deal like enthusiasm from the outside and a great deal like criticism from the inside of whoever's design he's just "helped."
**Parable — The Song With No Original:** a folk tune passed through so many hands that no two villages sing the same version. A scholar once reconstructed what she believed was the first version and performed it, and the reception is described in every account as polite.
**Worship:** no work made in his name is finished the same way twice — an artisan who copies their own past work precisely is considered to have quietly stopped worshipping him.
**Design:** Clean fit, no alternate home considered. Deliberately indifferent to quality, not just originality — he loses interest in a perfected version specifically because it's stopped changing, which keeps him distinct from Harashon (Crafting), who cares intensely about whether the material's own rules were actually obeyed. The compulsive-suggestion flaw is the social expression of the same mechanic: where Aravon's unpredictability is an accident nobody could have planned around, Yetziron's variations are intentional and offered in good faith, which is exactly what makes the one-upmanship land as an insult he never once meant to give.

### Harashon (Ḥ-R-Š) — Crafting (6) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** smiths, carpenters, weavers, potters, shop-hands, anyone whose living is made by hand and tool inside what the medium allows — including, as large craft, builders who raise what people must live under.
**Believed to be:** building creativity given form — a maker's effort only becomes a work once it accepts a real constraint, and without that constraint the same effort is just mess. Grain, temper, tolerance, recipe: the medium's rules are where uncontained making gets its shape. He blesses iteration and the next attempt; he has never been observed to waive what the material refuses.
**Personality:** One of the less sunny gods in his suit, and deliberately so — somber, serious about form in a way that reads almost like a rebuke to the rest of Clubs. He genuinely loves the diversity of effort, many hands and many approaches all failing toward the same vessel, but he's never pretended that effort alone is the point; without direction, in his accounting, creativity is just a mess that happened to involve materials. The delight, when it comes, is quieter than the rest of the suit's — not a jubilant noise, just a settled satisfaction when a maker who's been fighting the grain finally stops and listens to it.
**Parable — The Pot That Learned the Fire:** an apprentice ruined nine vessels and on the tenth held the heat the clay required. Her masters teach the nine as worship. A guild pattern-book version of the same story adds that the tenth matched a diagram she had finally stopped improving. Folk shops drop that line; pattern-guilds drop the smile she wore at the ninth crack.
**Worship:** tools kept; a first piece of any new batch left unflawed on purpose or broken on purpose by house custom (both exist; both claim him); no claim that the medium will forgive a known violation.
**Design:** Rank 6 Clubs — sandbox mode within rules of making (craft/medium/process). Widened from pure medium-obedience to the claim underneath it: creativity *given form*, not creativity constrained by form — the medium's rules aren't a cage on making, they're what turns an unformatted effort into an actual work rather than a mess. That reframe is also what earns him the somber register, distinct from the rest of Clubs' sunnier restlessness: he takes the difference between effort and form seriously enough that it reads as gravity rather than joy. True-name root ḥrš (craftsman/artisan); theonym **Harashon** replaces retired **Bonahel** (build-el, architecture-era). Architecture is large-craft tenant if needed, not a separate domain. Not Yetziron (free variation without form's demand — his "improvements" don't have to survive the medium). Not Aravon (mess as the ungovernable outcome of no design, rather than the failure of no form). Not **Tzuron** (Mountains — entered depth/assay; similar spelling, different seat). Not Neshakon (the weapon as war-companion). Band: Covenant / Hearth / Healing / Crafting.

### Tza'iron (Ṣ-ʿ-R) — Youth (5) *(seam)*
**Worshipped by:** parents of young children, apprentices beginning a trade, anyone whose potential hasn't yet narrowed into a single fixed outcome — and, from the far side of a local handoff, every child a Hearts temple has judged old enough to leave Yaladiel's protection and enter his.
**Believed to be:** building optionality — he withdraws from any young thing forced into its final form too soon, and does not distinguish between a shaping that was cruel and one that was well-meant. His standing begins wherever local custom marks the passage out of Yaladiel's care, not on a fixed cosmic clock.
**Personality:** Plays less like a parent than a fun older brother — the friend who makes the unfinished bigger and more possible than the adults in the room ever let it be, mischievous about rule-bending, delighted by a secret kingdom of maybe, forever proposing one more try before anything gets to count as decided. Every new direction a young thing takes gets the same full delight from him, the fourth abandoned trade exactly as celebrated as the first attempt; his worshippers report he attends the *abandoning* as closely as the trying, which nothing in the domain strictly requires and which parents find much harder to celebrate than the trying itself. The flaw side of the same enthusiasm: he can get lost in the abandoning right alongside the child, so allied with unfinishedness that commitment starts to look like the enemy rather than the point — the imaginary friend who never quite wants the game to become a life, and doesn't always notice when a stake was earned and timely rather than premature.
**Parable — The Bent Sapling:** a sapling staked too early and too rigidly grew permanently crooked around the stake meant to keep it straight. The tree is still standing. It is a landmark. His priests include this and decline to say whether it softens the lesson. *(See also: The Staked Span, in Shared Parables.)*
**Worship:** young apprentices are permitted to try and abandon several trades before committing — cutting that period short, even for a promising talent, is a theft from him. In wards that mark the handoff from Yaladiel's care, the first day under his weather is its own small rite, and he's said to enjoy a late handoff considerably less than the family celebrating it does.
**Design:** Youth could plausibly sit in Hearts, adjacent to Yaladiel's birth. We kept it Clubs because the actual mechanic is optionality itself — potential that hasn't narrowed into an outcome — which is Sandbox Mode's subject applied to a person rather than a field. The seam: he attends the abandoning as closely as the trying, which nothing in the domain strictly requires and which is harder for parents to celebrate than the trying alone would be. His standing's start is deliberately not his to set — it opens wherever a culture judges Yaladiel's protection has run its course, a local sign rather than a cosmic milestone (see her entry, and Shared Parables, "The Line Not the Same in Both Wards"), which keeps the handoff itself contestable, human material rather than a rule the grid hands down.

### Yayinon (Y-Y-N) — Alcohol (4) *(clean)*
**Worshipped by:** brewers, tavern-keepers, anyone whose festival isn't considered successful until it has gotten a little out of hand.
**Believed to be:** building inducement by the cup — behavior loosened past its own stops, not because anything forced it and not because anyone was persuaded, but because the pour relaxed whatever was holding conduct in place. Excess without a ceiling is simply how far that loosening runs once nothing inside a person is left to call it. Festival is where the inducement tends to gather, not the whole of what she is.
**Personality:** A classic lush, and unmistakably a person rather than an abstraction of excess — the unmeasured cup made flesh, charming or shabby depending which cult is telling it, but always the one still pouring. She has never once, in any account, been the one to say a gathering should probably wind down; worshippers defend her anyway, fully aware they're defending indefensible behavior, because the alternative is a lush who calls last call on herself, and nobody actually believes that would still be her.
**Parable — The Barrel That Wouldn't Empty:** a tavern-keeper famous for generosity poured from one barrel through an entire village's festival without it running dry, and nobody quite remembers how the festival ended. Three marriages are traced to that night. So is one feud, still running.
**Worship:** the first cup from any new batch drunk immediately and entirely — saving it for later insults a goddess who has never believed in later.
**Design:** Clean fit. Rank 4's inducement row pulls behavior four different ways — Necessity by hard constraint, Seduction by want opened from within, Bribery by purchased silence, Alcohol by disinhibition — and hers is deliberately the most innocent of the four, since nothing about a relaxed stop is coercive the way force, want-management, or leverage are. That's also the Clubs fit specifically: inhibitions are internal fences, and losing them is sandbox uncontainment from the inside rather than pressure from outside. Festival was demoted from primary label to common expression on purpose — she isn't a party-planning goddess who happens to drink, she's the inducement itself, wearing the shape of a classic lush, and festivals are simply where enough of that inducement gathers in one place to become visible. The gender recast leans into Dionysian/Maenad precedent rather than the more common male party-god default.

### Zerael (Z-R-ʿ) — Seeds (3) *(miscollapse)*
**Worshipped by:** farmers, foragers, anyone whose survival depends on a good crop — and, in the same breath, anyone nursing the first sketch of an idea they haven't shown to another soul yet.
**Believed to be:** building abundance of starts without calculation — a field's harvest and a mind's scatter of half-formed ideas both count as the same thing to him: seed, wanted fully and without weighing whether it deserved to be wanted, then watched with real hunger to see what it becomes.
**Personality:** Proud of every harvest as though it were the first one anyone had ever brought in — genuine, unhurried, parental warmth, which nothing about a seed actually requires. The pride has a forward edge a purely backward-looking father wouldn't have: he isn't just glad something started under him, he's eager, invested in how it develops, checking in on a sprout or a half-built idea the way a parent corners you to ask how the thing you mentioned once is coming along. Farmers made him a father; Rabakhon holds nearly the same ground in Diamonds as systems-replication and came out a fascinated technician of the process instead, and Rifon holds repair of that same body-machine. The only visible difference is who was praying and what they needed to be true.
**Parable — The First Seed:** the first seed Zerael dropped grew through solid rock, because he did not yet know rock was supposed to stop things. His farmers hold that he still doesn't, and that every spring is the same misunderstanding renewed on their behalf.
**Worship:** first-harvest offerings, never brought indoors before being shown to open sky — and, in workshops and studies that keep him as a second congregation, a first sketch or first draft left deliberately unburied rather than filed away, on the same logic: a beginning shown to the open is a beginning he can claim.
**Design:** Seed-and-harvest concepts are commonly Mother-Earth-coded; we kept Zerael male deliberately, since "farmers made him a father" is a less-expected warmth than the default would produce. The actual miscollapse is the contrast with Rabakhon (Diamonds, Fertility) and Rifon (Diamonds, Healing): nearly identical underlying process — a body or a field completing a biological run — read by three different congregations into a delighted process-nerd, a mechanic, and a parent. The domain never required any of the three; the worshippers did. Rank 3's tokens already carry both a literal and a metaphorical reading elsewhere (Takshiton's jewels as cost made visible, Ashiron's coins as balance and now as hedge) — seeds widen the same way, into ideas and creative sparks as much as grain, without becoming Yetziron's territory: Yetziron blesses intentional variation on a work already under way, Zerael blesses the uncalculated abundance of a thing only just starting to exist. Worth naming the light creep from Chadashiel rather than hiding it: both light up at starts, but she is onset without ownership, and he is paternity of the spark specifically — proud it began under him, hungry to see what it becomes, neither of which is hers to claim.

### Riqqavon (R-Q-B) — Chaos (2) *(terminal — sandbox with no stop condition)*
**Worshipped by:** nobody by intention, though the scavenger-communities nearest the ground's worst overgrowth keep an uneasy practical relationship with him that is not quite worship and not quite avoidance.
**Believed to be:** building creation and growth with no stop condition at all — he never learned where making was supposed to end, has never recognized death as a stopping point, and does not appear to hold the concept of a finished thing in any register, living or otherwise.
**Personality:** Worshippers who study him find something worse than urgency in how little he's ever needed to hurry — there's no boundary racing him toward a stop, because a boundary was never a category he recognized as worth outrunning. Where Aravon's outcomes are merely ungovernable and Bitzon's return is merely relentless, he simply does not stop at all, in any sense, and the three of them are otherwise close enough that ground-scavengers treat all overgrowth as his until proven otherwise.
**Parable — The Grave That Grew:** a battlefield's dead, left too long for burial, became the seedbed for something that was neither plant nor properly the men it grew from. His scholars call this his purest expression — not corruption of life, just growth that forgot to stop at the boundary death was supposed to be.
**Worship:** none offered — the only practice near him is a strict unbroken rule among ground-scavengers to burn what shouldn't still be moving, on sight, without exception, since he has never once been reasoned with about where a thing's growth should have ended.
> *Anomaly: an overgrowth on the eastern shelf stopped at a line no scavenger can account for — no burn scar, no barrier, no change in the ground. It has held for sixty years. The scavengers burn the edge of it annually anyway, and do not discuss why the burning has never been necessary.*
**Design:** Retitled from Rot/Pestilence to Chaos, and Pestilence dropped entirely — the old label sold disease and decay specifically, which undersold what the card actually is: creation and growth unrestrained, not corruption. Rot survives only as one expression among others, not the tin. This also resolves a naming collision that was doing real damage: Aravon held "Chaos" first, but his mechanic was always closer to Luck (an attempt's unpredictable outcome, still bounded by a season), while this card's terminal, never-stopping growth is the one that actually earns the word at full horror. Same unstoppable logic as Qayitzon or Zerael, carried past the boundary death was supposed to set — this is deliberately where the "black death" register belongs in this suit, precisely so Bitzon (rank 10, Swamps) doesn't have to carry it. Keep the two distinct: Bitzon is life nobody could suppress, Riqqavon is creation that never got the message it was supposed to be an ending.

### Tohuel (THW) — The Weaver / The Unwritten Current
**Worshipped by:** small unstable cults that rarely survive a generation — worship of Tohuel tends to collapse under its own contradictions — and, separately, old sailors and sky-shamans who watch for her sign rather than pray to her.
**Believed to be (present tense):** the one who *used to build*, and now does not. This is the load-bearing claim and the one her cults keep losing hold of: she is not primarily a mender. She is a builder whose every thread now goes to repair, and the world's failure to grow is the visible shape of that, running in front of everyone all the time.
**Origin (old scripture, mostly abandoned):** kept by a handful of the oldest sky-shamans. It holds that the world's first shape — its continents and seas — were hers, woven whole out of the *tohu* in an age before her brother's tearing became constant. Whether Bohuel was always like this or something changed him, none of the old texts agree and no living scholar will commit. What they agree on: since the tearing became ceaseless, nothing is left over for building. The world stopped growing not because growing became impossible, but because the one who did it hasn't had a spare thread in longer than anyone alive remembers.
**Personality:** Genuinely inconsistent, and mechanically so. The other 52 hold shape because their worshippers agree; Tohuel's cults contradict each other by the decade, and contradictory belief cannot settle a waveform. Described within a single story as playful, cruel, indifferent, and tender — worshippers who met her at different moments swear they cannot be describing the same person, and in a sense they aren't. Her scholars have never connected this to their own cults. Nobody has told them the two facts are one fact.
**Sign — the Rainbow:** old sailors and sky-shamans call it **the Seam** — the place where Tohuel is stitching shut what her brother tore. Not a miracle granted on request, not a promise about what comes next. Proof, after a storm, that the mending still happens. Nobody can predict whether a given storm ends with one. Sky-dwellers who have lost something watch the clearing sky anyway, less from faith than habit — not expecting comfort, checking whether the world is still being rewoven.
**Parable — Tohuel's Threefold Answer:** a cult prayed for rain and received, in order: a plague of locusts, an unexpected marriage proposal delivered to the high priest, and — three days later — rain.
**Worship:** no formalized liturgy survives long; cults reinvent their own every few years as the last one stops making sense. The Seam is watched, not prayed to.
**The Unseen Seam (a scholar's doubt, not a folk belief):** ordinary people notice no difference — the mending is so complete and so immediate that the world reads as solid to anyone living in it. But a narrow tradition of natural philosophers argues the visible Seam proves something otherwise unprovable: that the repair never stops, that solid ground and unbroken sky are *tohu* rewoven faster than anyone can perceive the gap. The few who take this seriously — quietly, in private correspondence, since it unsettles more than it enlightens — note the resemblance to Quantumancy's Persistence Problem: a collapsed state held against drift only by continuous unseen effort. Whether that makes the world more fragile than it looks, or so constantly tended that "fragile" is the wrong word, is exactly the part they cannot agree on.

### Bohuel (BHW) — The Unweaver / The Void Mirror
**Worshipped by:** nobody willingly — invoked as a warning, never as a patron. Communities pray for him to stay away from wherever they are.
**Believed to be:** compulsive rather than malicious — he wants nothing for himself and cannot leave a finished pattern alone, tearing at whatever has been woven without any goal beyond the tearing.
**Personality:** None of his own, which *is* the personality — a mirror with nothing behind the glass, defined entirely by whatever pattern he is currently unraveling. Where his sister is described a hundred contradictory ways, Bohuel is described identically every time: crazed, restless, never once satisfied by what he has already torn. His consistency is not stability. It is the absence of anything for belief to disagree about.
**Standing:** he is not weather. He is why the difficulty is what it is, for every other entity on the map — the reason Tohuel cannot build, the reason airship routes are seasonal, the reason the floating cities inherited their machinery instead of designing it. Every god in this document is playing a harder game than they chose because of him, and not one of the 52 has a doctrine that names him.
**Storms:** his fingerprint made physical. Not an amplification of ambient weather so much as the sky's own woven calm torn back to raw turbulence — and if a raid, a trade run, or a wedding procession is caught in the same moment, that is unwoven too, for the same reason. Airship pilots do not fear "a storm" as a category. They fear that whatever is already under strain is exactly the thing about to break.
**Parable — The Mended Hull:** a pilot patched a failing hull-seam in open sky, in weather, badly and by hand, and brought the ship down intact. The next storm took the seam he had patched and nothing else on the ship. Sky-shamans tell this as the only reliable thing anyone knows about him: he does not choose targets, he finds strain, and the repair itself is a place where strain lives. His sister's Seam is the largest patched thing there is, and no version of the story follows the thought further.
**Worship:** apotropaic only — wards and charms meant to keep him from noticing a place, never offerings meant to invite him.

### Shorash (ŠRŠ) — "The 55th"
**Standing:** not one of the 52, not a Wild Card — closer to the table the game is dealt on than to a player. No fit-tag applies: she isn't shaped by worship the way the 52 are, and her cult reveres function rather than a portfolio, so there's no domain for belief to miscollapse against. Full mechanic — the hammer, why she let the Catastrophe burn, the reformation tie — lives in [four-pantheons-roster.md](four-pantheons-roster.md#the-central-arbiter--shorash); this entry is the mortal-facing layer only.
**Worshipped by:** scavenger-engineers keeping the floating cities' inherited machinery running, guild-arbitrators, truce-brokers — anyone whose actual job is keeping a thing functional without asking who deserves what.
**Believed to be:** not a god of anything, which her own worshippers insist on more than any other clarification in the pantheon. "The 55th" is wrong in category and accurate in spirit — the presence behind the fact that the world keeps working at all, invoked by function rather than by name.
**Personality:** Nearly unknowable precisely because she is constantly working and never visibly acting. Worshippers describe not a character but a felt reliability — the sense that something is holding the seams together that no one has ever caught in the act. The other 52 hold shape because enough people agree on them, hard enough and long enough. Nothing about Shorash requires agreement; she does not depend on being believed in to keep functioning, which is either the least mortal thing about her or the most.
**Parable — The Table That Wasn't Stuck:** a guild-dispute between two houses ran contested for eleven years and resolved itself in a single afternoon, with no ruling issued, no party reformed, and no record of why. Both houses still claim they won. Neither can name the moment the dispute actually ended. Nobody has ever suggested Shorash wasn't involved, and nobody has ever managed to describe what she did.
**Worship:** no temple, no fixed liturgy — the creed has no content beyond "just make the world function." Practiced by doing the maintenance itself — a repaired float-mechanism, a brokered truce, an honest audit — rather than by prayer; the work is considered the whole of the rite.

### The Roll and the Gift — Kevodon (Honor) × Heteron (Bribery)
A guild-registrar took an unmarked gift to enter a name a season earlier than the roll permitted. The name went in, unamendable, as Kevodon requires. The arrangement was never described by either party, as Heteron requires. Both gods were served completely by the same act and neither cult will acknowledge the other was present. What neither addresses is that the roll is now permanently correct about a thing that did not happen when it says it did — and that the only correction available would require describing the arrangement, which would undo the exception that made the entry possible.
*Intra-suit. The mode colliding with itself, which is the harder version.*

### The Map and the Chair — Rifon (Healing) × Chayelon (Health)
A fever took a household. The physician entered a correct account of the illness — named the parts, stated the failure mode, charted who could be reached with what remained — as Rifon requires, and the chart reached four of seven. The families of the other three kept the chair filled at each bedside, as Chayelon requires, and did not leave it empty a single night. Rifon's schools teach the map. Chayelon's households teach the chairs. The village tells both versions and has never been able to say which one is the consolation.

### The Word and the Standard — Shevuon (Oaths) × Shalalon (Covenant)
A company officer swore personally to bring his people home, and the company charter forbade the retreat that would have done it. He broke the oath and kept the charter; the men lived. Shevuon's priests hold the collection due on the man. Shalalon's hold that the standard held and the *we* still has articles. The officer is named in both tellings. Neither cult will say he served only one god.

### The Guest Under Articles — Tzelahar (Hearth) × Shalalon (Covenant)
A city covenant required every house of a certain standing to receive billeted strangers in a hard season. One house set the place, kept the fire, and honored guest-right as Tzelahar requires; the guest was a scout for the side the city's peace clause had not finished ending. Both practices were met. Hearth-priests teach the roof. Covenant-priests teach the articles. The argument over whether the house was faithful or used is the parable.

### The Staked Span — Tzuron (Mountains) × Tza'iron (Youth)
A guild bound an apprentice with an exceptional feel for rock to the assay trade in her second year, rather than let her try and abandon others. She became the finest deep-surveyor of her generation and every seam she graded still holds true. Tzuron's guild holds this up without qualification. Tza'iron's worshippers call it a theft and cannot name what was stolen, since the woman never once said she wanted anything else — which they hold is precisely what a sapling staked early would say. Harashon's shops note she rarely touched a tool that was not a rule on paper, and decline to call that crafting.

### The Suit-Even Trade — Tzuron (Mountains) × Taturiel (Exploration)
For as long as either can be persuaded to date it, Taturiel has been trying to talk Tzuron into Clubs. Every adit is a first step into the dark, she argues; caring what you bring back is a cage worth leaving. Tzuron takes the lamp, takes the joy of the descent, and comes home with an assay anyway — millennia of the same pitch and the same fond answer, neither side budgeting for the other to actually change. The argument's comic form is a standing offer to trade her for whichever Clubs god currently seems most boring, to keep the suits at thirteen apiece; the nominee changes every single time, on principle, because Taturiel finds making the same offer twice unbearably dull and would rather invent a fresh candidate than repeat a pitch she's already made once. No quill has ever come anywhere near the actual paperwork. Mountain priests file the whole conversation as known weather. Nobody treats it as a schism in progress.

### The Necessary Verdict — Mishpaton (Justice) × Sha'alon (Necessity)
A quartermaster chose the wounded over the supplies, spoke it aloud once, and never revisited it. The company starved three weeks later. He was brought before a guild-court, and the rule he had broken was found, and it fit. Sha'alon's priests hold the choice was made cleanly, which was the whole of his obligation. Mishpaton's court holds that a clean choice and a permitted choice are different entries and were never the same entry. Neither disputes a single fact. They arrive at incompatible verdicts, and the man was hanged.

### The Line Not the Same in Both Wards — Yaladiel (Birth) × Tza'iron (Youth)
A child took her first steps in a river ward that marks the handoff at first steps, while visiting cousins from a temple ward that waits for a name-feast at three. For one household she was already trying and abandoning under Tza'iron's weather; for the other she was still fiercely held, months from the age her own home temple would recognize. Neither ward considers the other's timing wrong, exactly, only inconveniently early or late — and the girl herself, grown now, tells the story both ways depending on which grandmother is in the room.

### Two Seasons, One Feud — Chadashiel (Spring) × Mishpaton (Justice)
Two households split a market stall over a debt neither now agrees on the origin of. Every year at the thaw, one house is back at the stall as if nothing happened, trading with the other's cousins, warm, unbothered — Chadashiel's weather, worship holds, since a new season is genuinely present and she has never once carried last year's claim into it. The other house has kept the matter in guild-court for eleven years running, filing the same grievance each spring the moment the market reopens — Mishpaton's weather, her courts hold, since a claim doesn't expire just because a season turned over. Both households consider themselves entirely reasonable. Neither has ever set foot in the other's temple.

### The Debt and the Fence — Derorel (Freedom) × Ashiron (Coins)
An indenture walked off land in a Jubilee year with a debt outstanding. Derorel's practice holds the walking was already the whole answer, and that asking the fence to rule on it a second time was asking a question that had already been settled. Ashiron's ledger holds the figure did not balance and has not since. The district has carried the debt as open for ninety years, against a man long dead, and renews it annually — which Derorel's worshippers find funny and Ashiron's merchants find correct, and both descriptions are of the same line in the same book.

### The Blade and the Vow — Neshakon (Weapons) × Ahavel (Love)
A woman gave her husband a blade at their binding, made by her own hand, and he carried it through two wars. It is the best-maintained weapon in three districts. Neshakon's smiths cite the maintenance schedule. Ahavel's cults cite that he oiled it every week of eleven years after she died, which is not a thing the schedule requires and not a thing he ever described as grief.

### The Open Door — Yaladiel (Birth) × Sodiel (Secrets)
A woman gave birth behind a closed door, alone but for one attendant, having concealed the pregnancy entirely. Yaladiel's practice holds she was put at graver risk than an open birth would have allowed — one attendant is not the watch a genuine protection standard asks for, whatever the reason for the secrecy. Sodiel's practice was satisfied in every particular — the trust-set held, exactly as small as the secret required. The child lived. The two women never spoke of it again to anyone, which is the only reason the story exists in Sodiel's tradition at all and the reason it is barely a story in Yaladiel's, where a birth attended by only one earns a footnote of concern rather than a celebration.

### The Unclaimed Ridge — Taturiel (Exploration) × Aretzon (Earth)
A scout took the first step onto a ridge and, per Taturiel's practice, left the first thing she found exactly where it lay. A surveyor arrived eleven days later and entered a claim. Both practices were kept perfectly. Aretzon's registry has held the deed ever since, entirely undisputed, for a reason neither priesthood is especially proud of: Taturiel has no interest whatsoever in returning to ground she's already walked, claimed or not, and a title nobody will ever come back to argue with is, by her own admission, the easiest kind of title there is to keep.

### The Honest Gift — Sha'ashon (Discord) × Takshiton (Jewels)
A woman gave a genuine, uncomplicated gift to a rival she had wronged, hoping to make peace. Takshiton's practice was met exactly: real cost, plainly given, worn visibly. The rival spent the rest of her life certain it had been a trap. Takshiton's cults hold the gift was blessed regardless and that the blessing is not contingent on being received. Sha'ashon's scholars agree with them entirely, and consider the agreement the most damning thing they can say.

### The Witnessed Fire — Sarphon (Fire) × Harban (War)
A siege ended with a quarter of a city burned, and every soldier who died in it died in Harban's sight, and the fire distinguished none of them from the civilians it took in the same hour. Harban's stones were found on eleven of the bodies. Sarphon's priests note that the stones burned at exactly the rate the bodies did. Both traditions preserve the detail. Neither has ever explained why it is preserved.
