## Regional Structure

Ya'arnahal's settled valley sits between two roughly parallel mountain ranges of Himalayan/Karakoram scale, running **west-east** — large enough to redirect, not merely be crossed by, continental glaciation. This isn't a narrow pass-through corridor; it's a genuinely wide valley in its own right, more mega-Himalayan basin than mountain gap, itself elongated west-east between the two ranges (consistent with the west-to-east prevailing weather, the far-west mega-lake, and Nahal Junction-west/Kitzigen-Falls-east layout of the settled road). The escarpment (see below) cuts across the valley transversely — running north-south, perpendicular to the valley's length — dividing it into a western portion (ancient, unglaciated) and an eastern portion (glaciated, younger). The scale explains an otherwise puzzling detail: the escarpment faces east, implying glacial approach from that direction rather than the expected north. The resolution is that the bounding ranges force the glacial trough to snake hundreds of miles east before curving back north, off-map — local escarpment orientation doesn't need to match the ice's true regional origin.

*Note: the Climate section below still describes "the bounding ranges" as "chiefly north/east" — worth reconciling against this west-east pairing (north range + south range) when next revisited; not resolved here to avoid guessing at an intent not yet stated.*

## The Escarpment

**Not a mountain-range-scale feature in its own right.** The escarpment is a glacial scouring high-water mark — an internal boundary *within* the wide valley itself, marking how far an ancient glacier advanced up the valley before stopping, not a separate range or one of the valley's outer walls. It still produces real relief (cliffs, the passes below, the falls at Kitzigen Falls) — glacial plucking and scouring routinely leave dramatic steps exactly like this — but categorically it's a scour line, not a mountain barrier. A hard geological line dividing two distinct ages of land:

- **Upper valley** — ancient, unglaciated terrain. Never scoured, meaning forest had geological-scale time to mature into the dense native woodland that dominates it. This is the older, first-settled, twin-river (Tigris-Euphrates-style) country already established in the world bible. **Corrected placement: Green's Grant** — a grassland clearing within this ancient woodland, functioning as a rest stop along the main arterial road between Nahal Junction and Kitzigen Falls — sits here, in the *upper* reaches of Maple Valley. This means Maple Valley itself spans both terrain types: its headwaters reach up into the ancient upper-valley woodland (Green's Grant), while the Ratkowski homestead, further down the valley, sits in the younger, glacier-scoured lower terrain (see below). Not two separate places sharing a name by coincidence — one valley, two ages of land.
- **Lower valley** — younger, glacier-scoured terrain. Stripped to bedrock/outwash in relatively recent glacial periods (geological-time "recent," not historical), producing rougher, more open ground — patchier young-growth forest, exposed rock, the actual Yukon-register terrain. This is the harsher, later-settled, single-trunk-river country the protagonist's claim sits in.

The two water systems converge at the escarpment itself, at **Kitzigen Falls** — twin rivers from the upper valley drop the escarpment together into the single trunk river below, with a small lake forming at the base as a plunge pool.

### The Passes

The escarpment itself runs roughly north-south (consistent with its east-facing scarp — a north-south cliff line naturally faces east or west, not north or south). Three ways across it, north to south:

- **Schweddy Pass (north):** a wider cliff/face erosion collapse — steep, but manageable.
- **Kitzigen Falls (middle):** the confluence itself. Various paths wind up and down alongside the falls, making this the *easiest* traversal point on the escarpment — which is why the main arterial road crosses here rather than at either pass to the north or south.
- **Yanike Pass (far south):** where the escarpment merges back into the southern mountain range. Long, narrow, nearly impassable — the route of last resort, not the route of choice.

Good texture for travel logistics and detour/emergency-route stakes: if Kitzigen Falls is blocked, compromised, or dangerous for any reason, the alternative is a steep-but-doable pass to the north or a nearly-impassable one to the south, not a simple reroute.

**Corrected:** Kitzigen Falls and Nahal Junction are distinct locations, not the same place under two names. Nahal Junction sits at the *western terminus* of the main arterial road, in the fertile land between the twin rivers — not on the confluence itself. A second road T-intersects at Nahal Junction, running north-south, reaching settlements built directly on each of the two rivers. The main arterial road continues east from Nahal Junction, crossing the escarpment at (or near) Kitzigen Falls — and that is effectively the end of the road. **The lower valley has no maintained road.** Not enough traffic in the backcountry justifies cutting and keeping one open; the single trunk river below the Falls — not large enough for real ships, but shallow-draft rafts and barges are plausible, giving it genuine cargo capacity — is the real transport backbone there, with informal overland trails as the only alternative. Likely reading: new arrivals land at Nahal Junction first (the safe, administrative anchor at the head of the road), travel east to Kitzigen Falls by road, and continue into the backcountry by river or trail from there — a genuine mode change at the Falls, not just a terrain change. Not yet fully confirmed, but consistent with everything else established.

## Climate

Prevailing weather moves west to east, largely unobstructed on the valley's western side (the bounding ranges are chiefly north/east, redirecting the glacial trough rather than walling off the west). This keeps the valley's baseline climate ordinary and survivable — real seasonal weather, not a rain-shadow gimmick.

### Base Condition

No meaningful axial tilt. Climate instead sits at a balance point on the freeze threshold — stable but not guaranteed, held there by the mega-lake's evaporation cycle (see below) rather than by seasonal tilt.

**"Winter" is settler terminology, not a claim about orbital mechanics.** Same device already established for place names (see [AI Refresher — Tone](../meta/AI_REFRESHER.md)): Earthers call a sustained stretch of persistent, non-melting snow cover "winter" because that's the word they have for it — regardless of whether a true axial-tilt season produces it. "Regular" in the Winter Scale table below describes how commonly and reliably homesteaders experience and plan around this state, not a fixed calendar cycle. No orbital mechanism is required or implied.

### The Feedback Risk

The balance is an ice-albedo tipping point, not a fixed equilibrium: a small perturbation (a weak evaporation stretch, a colder lake surface, a run of lower insolation) increases snow cover → higher reflectivity → less absorbed heat → the cold deepens further. Self-reinforcing, but — this is the important part — **a brake on recovery, not a second engine.** The loop delays the reset, it doesn't prevent it: cold event → snow accumulates → reflects more sun → warming suppressed → snow lingers longer → eventually enough exposed ground/water returns → warming wins → normal climate resumes. Ya'arnahal sits naturally on the warm side of this boundary; the equilibrium is stable enough that it does return. The open question is only ever *how long the return takes*, not *whether*. Author's-notes framing: **thermally stable, climatologically excitable.**

### The Winter Scale

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | White Fangs / Stormwall Burps | Common | 2–3 weeks | See [Stormwall Troughing](#stormwall-troughing) and [The White Fangs](#the-white-fangs-settler-name-for-a-stormwall-trough-event) below for full detail. A "Stormwall Burp" is multiple fangs active along the front at once — locally severe, survivable with ordinary competence. |
| 2 | Normal Winter | Regular | 3–8 months | The valley's standard hard season. Substantial accumulating snow, sustained cold. What a competent homesteader plans and stocks for. |
| 3 | Generational Winter | Rare (once or twice a lifetime) | Single severe trigger event, weeks to a hard season | Not one continuous years-long storm — a single acute event, followed by ordinary Scale 2 winters that fail to fully reset because the albedo baseline already shifted. The initial event is the loud crisis (empties marginal claims, kills livestock, enters oral history); what follows is the quieter, more dreadful part — snowpack that doesn't melt out, springs that don't really arrive, a community realizing the world hasn't gone back to normal after surviving the storm itself. Can plausibly break the loop (an unusually warm/wet season, increased lake evaporation) before it locks into Scale 4 — real dramatic uncertainty, not a fixed outcome. |
| 4 | Geological Winter | Extremely rare (~every 5,000 years) | Decades to centuries+ | True climate-shift, civilization-resetting if one hits during a settled period — what it looks like when the feedback loop genuinely runs away rather than breaking. **Deep background only, never meant to be witnessed on the page** — exists to give the whole system real underlying fragility, consistent with the "world is big, the story is here" principle already governing what stays undeveloped (see Unbuilt/Off-Map below). |

Scale 4 requires a genuinely extraordinary statistical run, not a different physical regime — something like millennia of unfavorable conditions compounding, not a different climate ceiling.

The hazard comes from what happens when air is forced up and over the Himalayan-scale peaks: adiabatic cooling at extreme altitude, followed by a fast, severe cold-air descent on the lee side — a katabatic-style event more extreme than the ordinary föhn effect, since the air's been chilled too far first to warm meaningfully on the way down. This produces sudden, dangerous cold snaps distinct from normal winter hardship — a recurring, foreshadowable hazard old-timers would recognize and newcomers wouldn't. Good candidate for demonstrating earned competence (see [Emotional Core](../README.md)) — recognizing the signs is exactly the kind of frontier knowledge that separates someone who's survived a season from someone who hasn't.

### Stormwall Troughing

The baseline katabatic effect above is a property of the peaks generally, but the *dangerous* version has a specific mechanism and a specific direction. Maple Valley abuts a jet-stream-analogous upper circulation system that can trough down over **Stormwall** (the northern bounding range), driving severe storms south into the valley. This is the primary, expected threat — a standing hazard that can arrive at any time, not a seasonal certainty, which is exactly what makes it dangerous rather than merely harsh. Old-timers watch Stormwall's skies the way sailors watch a barometer.

The same troughing can happen off the southern range, but it's a genuine anomaly there — rare enough that a southern event catching even experienced settlers flat-footed is plausible, precisely because the local safety culture is built around watching the north. This is also why the northern range earned a name (**Stormwall**) while the southern one hasn't (**South Range**, plain by comparison) — the asymmetry in naming reflects a real asymmetry in how much these two features matter to daily survival, not an unfinished naming pass.

### The White Fangs (settler name for a Stormwall Trough event)

**Mechanism:** The descending cold air doesn't come down as a broad front — it drains along the path of least resistance through specific low passes *in Stormwall itself* (a different, much larger set of passes than the three escarpment crossings — Schweddy, Kitzigen Falls, Yanike — which cross the internal glacial-scour boundary, not the bounding range; don't conflate the two). Prevailing westerlies off the tepid mega-lake are forced up and over Stormwall, cool drastically at altitude (adiabatic cooling), then descend too fast and too cold to rewarm on the way down.

**Behavior:** Narrow and localized, not valley-wide — a White Fang funnels through a specific pass, meaning some claims sit in a known run-out path and some don't. Old-timers know which passes produce them and which ground is downstream; newcomers don't. On arrival, the cold air instantly drops the dew point of any warmer air ahead of it, producing a visible, advancing wall of ice-fog and flash rime — trees, ground, exposed surfaces frosting over almost in real time. The hazard announces itself visually before the cold arrives, giving anyone who recognizes the sign a narrow warning window.

**Danger:** Beyond the extreme cold itself, instant-rime is its own specific threat — moisture on skin, breath, or wet clothing can freeze essentially on contact, sharper and more particular than generic hypothermia risk. Survival response is concrete and learnable (cover exposed skin, get under shelter, don't get caught wet-handed) — exactly the kind of local knowledge a homesteader accumulates over a season and a newcomer has to learn the hard way. Strong candidate scene material for the "earned competence" emotional core.

**Naming:** Settler-coined, after the phenomenon's visual character — a jagged white front bearing down out of the treeline — rather than any formal terminology. Consistent with the standing convention that Ya'arnahal place/feature names are settler shorthand, not official record (see [AI Refresher — Tone](../meta/AI_REFRESHER.md)): the name arose from what the thing does to people, the same way Kitzigen Falls, Schweddy Pass, and Green's Grant did. A deliberate, acknowledged nod to Jack London on the author's part — earned because the mechanism was fully worked out on its own terms first; it reads as unremarkable in-world terminology to a reader who doesn't catch the reference, and as a quiet wink to one who does.

## The Mega-Lake (off-map, far west)

A body of water on the scale of Lake Baikal combined with the Great Lakes, feeding the valley's weather system as its moisture source. Fed by glacial meltwater driven deep underground near the mountain roots and geothermally warmed in transit (plausible given the crustal thickening implied by ranges this large), the lake runs unusually warm — "tepid" — rather than frigid despite its glacial origin. This drives an active evaporation cycle, supplying the moisture that the prevailing westerlies carry into the valley and that the mountains wring out as orographic precipitation (and occasionally violent cold air) on the near side.

This closes a complete climate loop: glacier → geothermal warming → tepid mega-lake → active evaporation → westerly winds → orographic precipitation/cold-snap hazard on the peaks → valley weather. It also explains why Ya'arnahal supports dense forest and real river systems at all rather than being a barren cold-desert lee — the moisture source justifies the ecology already established (Neomaples, conifer forest, the Cottage Oak's cold-buffering adaptation).

## Sky

Two suns: the primary working sun, and a small, distant white dwarf companion — a true (dim) light source rather than a reflector, so it never phases, and positioned such that it never eclipses. Contributes negligible heat; purely ornamental, deliberately subverting genre expectations (Tatooine-style double suns) rather than driving plot or climate. May accrue in-story folk significance/omens organically, but carries no mechanical weight. (Consistent with the world bible's existing Sky section — no change, this confirms it.)

## Unbuilt/Off-Map (deliberately)

The far side of the mountains, the full extent of the glacial trough, and the mega-lake's coastline all remain undeveloped by design — enough worked out to support a future Lewis-and-Clark-style expedition arc if wanted, without requiring it. Consistent with the "world is big, the story is here" principle carried over from Earth's own established scope philosophy (`earth/AI-REFRESHER.md`: "The story is Manchester. Not Earth. Not the multiverse.") — a deliberate cross-world consistency, not a coincidence.
