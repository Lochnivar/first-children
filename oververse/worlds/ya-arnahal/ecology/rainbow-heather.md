# Rainbow Heather

**What it is:** A common groundcover plant, ordinary-looking at a glance — low, woody, carpeting exposed ground in loose mats, one more example of how prosaic and Earth-familiar Ya'arnahal's flora can feel.
**Category:** Flora — Ya'arnahal, found in open clearings, sunny banks, and especially throughout lower Maple Valley
**Related:** [Trunk River Backcountry](../locations/regions/trunk-river-backcountry.md), [Ecology index](README.md)

---

## Overview

Its one striking departure from the mundane is bloom color, which is neither fixed nor uniform: individual plants, and even individual blooms on the same plant, can display wildly different colors, giving a wild bank of it a scattered, almost technicolor look.

## Mechanism

Bloom color is tied to trace elements and compounds present in the soil the plant is rooted in. Rainbow Heather isn't bred or cultivated for this trait — it's simply how the plant works in the wild, uptaking and reflecting local soil chemistry through pigment in its blooms. A wild patch growing over mixed or uneven soil composition will show a corresponding scatter of colors; the variation is the plant reading its environment, not a decorative trait for its own sake (consistent with the standing principle that native flora's useful traits are incidental to survival function, not evolved for human benefit — see [Milknut](milknut.md)).

One known correlation: nitrogen-rich soil produces a vibrant hunter green bloom.

## Discovery (In-Story)

Sarah, who loves heather, has **Nick** transplant some into a prepared flower bed. Regardless of the color it displayed in the wild — Nick's first transplant was copper red — it turns hunter green once established in the bed. They try transplanting differently colored wild specimens, and the result is consistent: whatever goes into the prepared bed eventually turns the same hunter green. Neither of them has a chemistry kit or reason to suspect soil composition at first — the likelier early theories are things like sunlight, watering, or some effect of no longer being wild. The connection to nitrogen-rich soil (prepared garden beds being heavily amended/fertilized) is something they, or the reader, may only piece together later.

## Practical Use (Downstream)

Because bloom color reliably tracks soil chemistry, once the connection is understood Rainbow Heather functions as an informal, living soil test — particularly for nitrogen. A settler with enough experience can glance at a border planting and get a rough read on soil health without any instruments. It's the kind of practical folk-knowledge a homesteading community would develop organically, plant by plant and season by season, rather than through any formal study — same register as reading Cottage Oak health or hummingbee colony vigor.
